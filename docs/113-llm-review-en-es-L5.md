# en-es L5 — LLM review

Wave 1 of the level: `L5-M1` and `L5-M2`, the RANGE modules, authored under issue #573. This is the
first en-es L5 wave, so this file opens the level's review chain the way `docs/61` opened L2's,
`docs/77` opened L3's and `docs/95` opened L4's; later waves append their own `##` sections rather
than editing this one.

Every surface below was checked against the REAL emitted index, not against memory and not against
the briefs: `npm run content:owner -- en-es …`, reading `public/content/en-es/index/` as it stood on
2026-09-08, which reports **949 surfaces owned, folded over 40 modules through L4-M10** — the whole
of L1–L4, which is one level deeper than the fold `docs/104` was planned against. This wave built
nothing: `public/content/` is shared with eight sibling courses authoring their own L5, so the
strict build runs once, at collection. `maxSpan` is **3 and stays 3**: the longest surfaces either
module opens are `echar una mano`, `echar de menos`, `meter la pata`, `dar una vuelta`,
`poner al día`, `tomar el pelo`, `tener ganas de`, `en las nubes` and `no me digas`, three tokens
each, and every other new multi-token surface (`hecho polvo`, `hacer caso`, `ni idea`, `es decir`,
`es broma`, `hace gracia`, `qué va`, `qué morro`, `ni hablar`, `anda ya`, `en serio`) is two.

## Wave 1 — L5-M1, L5-M2 (#573)

### L5-M1 "Sayings and idioms" — the noun is the meaning and the verb is the part you cannot guess

Ten displays:

1. `¿Me puedes echar una mano? Es decir, ¿me puedes ayudar?`
2. `Echo de menos a mi hermana, pero la veo en agosto.`
3. `Metí la pata en la reunión, dije algo muy mal.`
4. `El médico lo dijo, pero no le hice caso.`
5. `Después de la reunión te puedo poner al día, si quieres.`
6. `Con este frío, es difícil tener ganas de salir de casa.`
7. `Estoy hecha polvo, no puedo dar una vuelta contigo hoy.`
8. `Hoy estoy en las nubes, no entiendo nada.`
9. `No tengo ni idea de este horario, lo siento.`
10. `Creo que me quieres tomar el pelo, no te creo.`

What it teaches, in twelve word rows and five module rules. The system stated is the LIGHT-VERB
COLLOCATION, not a list of sayings, exactly as the brief's note 2 demands: a nearly empty verb plus
a fixed noun, where the noun carries the meaning and the verb is chosen by the noun rather than by
the sense. Eight of the ten displays are one of those pairs — `echar una mano`, `echar de menos`,
`meter la pata`, `hacer caso`, `poner al día`, `tener ganas de`, `dar una vuelta`, `tomar el pelo` —
and rule 1 is the interference rule that makes the family learnable: English GIVES a hand, TAKES a
walk and PAYS attention, Spanish THROWS a hand, GIVES a turn and MAKES case, so translating the verb
is wrong every single time. Two displays are the second family, `estar` plus a fixed complement for
a passing state (`hecho polvo`, `en las nubes`), and rule 3 pins the two things a learner gets wrong
there: it is always `estar` and never `ser`, and `hecho polvo` agrees for gender (S07 ships `hecha`,
its variation `hecho`, so both are seen). The tenth pattern, `No + tener + ni idea + de + <N>`, is
S09.

The `<idiom clause> + , + <plain restatement clause>` pattern is the module's spine and needed a
hinge the course did not own, so `es decir` is a row of its own (S01) and reappears in the pool at
C12. Four of the ten displays restate themselves plainly — S01, S03, S08 and S10 — which is the brief's
"say the plain thing beside the figurative one" turned into a habit rather than an instruction.

Every `usage` line carries the frequency fact the brief's note 3 asks for, because an idiom learned
from a book gets over-used: rule 5 says out loud that these are neutral, unremarkable register and
that an idiom in every sentence marks a learner faster than a plain mistake does. Nothing here is
`refrán`, nothing is regional, nothing is vulgar, nothing is meant as its opposite, no diminutive
appears, and no accidental `se` appears — M2's, M3's, M7's and M8's territory respectively, all
left alone.

### L5-M2 "Humour and teasing" — the suffix is an attitude, and the tease is not finished until `es broma`

Ten displays:

1. `Un momentito, tío, que ya voy.`
2. `Vivo en una casita muy pequeña, un poquito lejos de aquí.`
3. `¿Quieres un cafelito? Anda, que es muy temprano.`
4. `Vaya cochazo tienes, tío. Es un coche muy grande.`
5. `Tengo un problemilla con el móvil, no es nada.`
6. `Mi hermano es un preguntón, un poco pesado, pero majo.`
7. `¡Qué móvil más feo tienes, tío! Es broma, es muy guay.`
8. `Tu hermano es muy gracioso, siempre me hace gracia.`
9. `No me digas, ¿en serio? Qué va, hombre.`
10. `Ni hablar, tía. Qué morro tienes, es una broma pesada.`

What it teaches, in twenty-four word rows and six module rules. The claim the module is built on is
the brief's note 2: the suffix marks how the SPEAKER FEELS about the thing and size is only one
reading among several. Five suffixed rows carry it — `momentito`, `casita`, `poquito`, `cafelito`
(-ito, warmth), `problemilla` (-illo, a shrug), `cochazo` (-azo, bulk and admiration) and
`preguntón` (-ón, bulk and mockery) — and each note points back at the plain word an earlier level
owns, which rule 3 states as a mechanical fact: a suffixed word is a NEW word, not a form. S02 puts
`casita` beside `muy pequeña` and S05 puts `problemilla` beside `problema` in the same breath, so
the learner can hear that the suffix is not the adjective.

The second system is the exclamative frame and the closed set of two-word denials. `Qué + N + más +
Adj` is S07 and `Vaya + N` is S04, both built out of words the course already owns (`qué`, `más`,
`vaya`) rather than out of twins; the denials are `qué va`, `no me digas`, `ni hablar` and `anda ya`
(S03, S09, S10), with `en serio` as their positive counterpart. `tío`/`tía` ships as one row with
both forms and a note that says how much less marked it is than the English words a learner maps
onto it.

Rule 5 is the interference rule and it is the module's reason for existing: English closes a tease
after the fact with *just kidding*, Spanish says `es broma` in the same breath, and S07 is
engineered so that its own `mistake` block is the identical sentence with the `es broma` removed —
grammatical, complete, and simply an insult about somebody's phone. The register decision is stated
in rule 6 and honoured in the file: `tú` throughout, every sentence chipped `informal`, no `usted`
frame anywhere, per `docs/53` §1 and the brief's note 4. No sarcasm, no vulgarity, no slang that
dates, and every `usage` line names who the line is for and who it is not for.

### Brief seams corrected, and what `content:owner` actually said

**1. `tener ganas de` is free only as an infinitive — the conjugated frame is L3-M6's.** The M1
brief's note 5 lists `tener ganas` and `tener ganas de` among the free surfaces, and that is
literally true, but it is not the fact an author needs. The forms a real sentence uses are already
owned:

```
tener ganas de    free
tener ganas       free
tengo ganas de    L3-M6
tienes ganas de   L3-M6
ganas             free
```

So the row ships with `forms: ["tener ganas de"]` and NO conjugation at all — a deliberate paradigm
HOLE of exactly the en-it `sarei`/`sarebbe` kind the wave brief warns about. A complete-looking
`tengo / tienes / tener` list would have opened two keys L3-M6 already owns and made two thirds of
the row unreachable. The note points back at L3-M6 by name, and S06's variation deliberately shows
`Hoy tengo ganas de dar una vuelta`, which resolves to L3-M6's note — the correct behaviour, since
that is where the learner met it. This is the single most load-bearing correction of the wave.

**2. A `refrán` cannot enter the comprehension pool, so no proverb appears in the shipped file at
all.** The brief's note 4 and `docs/104` §7 both say M1 treats the long `refrán` "as anything but
comprehension" / "as comprehension only". The pool is not a free-text field: `tools/check-shown.ts`
scans every `comprehensionPool` display with the same resolver it uses on a hero line, so a proverb
must have every one of its tokens taught. `A quien madruga, Dios le ayuda` does not:

```
quien     free
madruga   free
dios      free
ayuda     L2-M8
```

Three of its four lexemes are untaught, and minting them would be teaching vocabulary the module has
no other use for. The module therefore ships no `refrán` anywhere — not in a display, not in the
pool. The `refrán` remains a thing to NAME in prose, and `docs/104` §7's phrasing should be read as
"never as production", not as "put one in the pool".

**3. The brief's stated reason for killing `ser pan comido` and `costar un ojo de la cara` is not
the mechanical one.** Note 5 says `pan → L1-M3` and `cara → L1-M8` "between them kill
costar un ojo de la cara and ser pan comido as display items". The fold does not work that way.
`surfaceIndexKeys` gives a whole multi-token phrase its own key, and `matchSurfaces` walks
longest-first, so an owned single word never blocks a longer key containing it — this module proves
it three times over, shipping `dar una vuelta` while `dar` is L2-M1's, `tomar el pelo` while `pelo`
is L2-M2's, and `hecho polvo` while `hecho` is L3-M7's, all three clean:

```
dar             L2-M1        dar una vuelta    free
pelo            L2-M2        tomar el pelo     free
hecho           L3-M7        hecho polvo       free
pan             L1-M3        ser pan comido    free
cara            L1-M8        ojo               free
```

What actually rules out `costar un ojo de la cara` is `maxSpan`: it is six tokens and the index is
capped at three, so it could never be one key and its parts do not resolve. `ser pan comido` is
three tokens and WOULD have been indexable — it was left out on the brief's other, better ground
(the module is a system, not a phrasebook), not because `pan` is owned. Recorded so the next wave
does not repeat a rule that is not one.

**4. The restatement pattern needed a hinge the brief never mentions, and it was free.** Pattern 3
is `<idiom clause> + , + <plain restatement clause>` and the course owned no way to open the
restatement. `content:owner` said:

```
es decir   free
decir      L4-M3
```

`es decir` ships as M1's own two-token row. It is safe precisely because of the longest-match rule
in seam 3: the two-token key wins over bare `decir` wherever both could apply, and where `decir`
appears alone it still resolves to L4-M3.

**5. Bare `hace` is not taught, so `hace gracia` had to be the whole key.** The index owns
`hace falta` and `hace falta que` but not the verb form on its own:

```
hace         free
gracia       free
hace falta   L4-M4
hacer        L1-M6
```

`me hace gracia` therefore only resolves because `hace gracia` is indexed as a two-token surface;
had M2 taught `gracia` alone, the walk would have stopped on an unresolved `hace`. Same class of
trap as `hacer caso` in M1, which is indexed whole for the same reason.

**6. The M2 index seam held exactly as written, which is worth recording too.** All thirty-five
surfaces the brief's note 5 lists as free came back `free`, and the five it lists as owned came back
with the owners it names: `vaya → L3-M3`, `venga → L4-M4`, `hombre → L4-M5`, `listo → L2-M2`,
`claro → L1-M10`. So the exclamative and vocative frames extend those rows and mint no twins:
`Vaya cochazo` (S04) and `Qué va, hombre` (S09) both use the owned word and resolve to the owning
module's note. Every diminutive base the brief names is owned exactly as it says: `momento → L2-M1`,
`casa → L1-M5`, `café → L1-M1`, `coche → L4-M2`, `problema → L2-M8`, and `un poco → L1-M10` for
`poquito`. `preguntón`'s base is `preguntó`, which is **L3-M5**'s.

**7. Two surfaces the briefs name are deliberately NOT shipped, and stay free for later modules.**
`ahorita` is in the M2 brief's note 2 as an example of attitude-over-size, but it is American, and
note 4 sends the regional half of the level to M3; teaching it here would put a variety label on a
module that has no room to explain one. `de vez en cuando` is four tokens and cannot be a key at
`maxSpan` 3 — and its second token is a trap in itself, since `vez` is free but `una vez` is
L4-M10's, so a split would collide with a shipped key. Both are named in prose only.
`golpazo`, `tonto`, `guapo`, `gordo` and `risa` are likewise still `free` after this wave.

### Open questions for the native pass

Continuing the en-es chain, whose last number in `docs/104` is 79. Nothing existing is renumbered.
These are about the SENTENCES this wave ships, not about the briefs.

80. **`echar una mano` as the default request for help.** M1-S01 ships it as the ordinary way to ask
    a friend for help with something small and physical, commoner in speech than `ayudar`, and its
    `usage` says so. Confirm the frequency claim for Spain, and say whether `¿Me echas una mano?` —
    the bare present, which this module does not ship — is in fact the commoner shape than the
    `¿Me puedes …?` frame chosen here.

81. **`echar de menos` versus `extrañar`.** S02's `usage` says `echar de menos` is what Spain says
    and `extrañar` does the same job in much of Latin America. Confirm the split, and say whether a
    peninsular learner meeting `extrañar` would misread it, since this course teaches neither
    variety label until M3.

82. **`meter la pata` as genuinely neutral.** S03's `usage` claims there is no rudeness in it at all
    and that this surprises English speakers, who hear "put my foot in it" as at least colloquial.
    Confirm it is sayable at work, to a manager, about yourself.

83. **`le hice caso`, not `lo hice caso`.** S04's note pins the person heeded as an indirect object.
    Confirm this holds in `leísta` and `loísta` regions alike, and whether `no le hice caso` or
    `no le hice ni caso` is what is actually said.

84. **`poner al día` of a person versus `actualizar`.** S05's `mistake` claims `actualizar` used of
    a person sounds like a software release note. Confirm, and confirm that `ponerse al día` for
    one's own backlog — named in `usage` but not shipped as a row — is as frequent as the
    transitive.

85. **The paradigm hole at `tener ganas de`.** M1 ships the infinitive alone and leaves
    `tengo/tienes ganas de` to L3-M6. Confirm that a learner meeting `Con este frío, es difícil
    tener ganas de salir de casa` reads it as ordinary rather than as bookish, and that the
    infinitive after `es difícil` is the natural frame rather than `dan ganas de`.

86. **`hecho polvo` and its gender agreement.** S07 ships `hecha polvo` in the hero and `hecho
    polvo` in the variation. Confirm the feminine is used as freely as the masculine and that the
    fixed noun `polvo` never agrees, and say whether `estar hecho polvo` carries any physical-labour
    connotation that would make it odd after an exam (the variation's setting).

87. **`en las nubes` against `on cloud nine`.** S08's `trap` insists the two are unrelated — one is
    inattention, the other happiness. Confirm there is no reading of `estar en las nubes` that
    means elated, in Spain or elsewhere.

88. **`ni idea` with a stranger.** S09's `usage` says the bare `Ni idea` is a shrug and needs
    softening with `lo siento` for a stranger. Confirm, and confirm that `No tengo ni idea` is
    stronger than `no lo sé` rather than merely more casual.

89. **`tomar el pelo` as warm rather than accusatory.** S10's `usage` calls it warm and everyday,
    naming the teasing rather than resenting it — which is what lets M2 build on it. Confirm the
    tone, and confirm that `¿Me estás tomando el pelo?`, which this module does not ship, is not
    sharper than the shipped `¿Me quieres tomar el pelo?`.

90. **`momentito` said to an adult stranger.** M2-S01's `usage` says `momentito` alone stays
    perfectly polite once `tío` is dropped. Confirm it is usable in a shop or an office with someone
    you do not know, since it is the module's only claim that a diminutive is register-neutral.

91. **`cafelito` and the inserted `l`.** S03's note explains `cafelito` rather than `cafeíto`.
    Confirm the form, and confirm the claim that offering a `cafelito` to a stranger assumes a
    closeness `café` does not.

92. **`anda` coaxing versus `anda ya` disbelieving.** S03 ships `anda` as an invitation and the row
    is keyed on `anda ya`, whose note says the `ya` reverses the job. Confirm the reversal is that
    clean, and that a learner adding `ya` to sound friendly really does land the opposite.

93. **`problemilla` as polite understatement.** S05's `usage` says a `problemilla` can be a serious
    problem being handled calmly. Confirm, and confirm the masculine article is never in doubt for a
    native (`un problemilla`).

94. **`preguntón` as teasing rather than insulting.** S06's note claims `-ón` mocks without
    insulting. Confirm this holds of `preguntón` said to somebody's face, and say whether the
    module's ordering rule — tease first, `majo` last — is how a native would actually build the
    line.

95. **`pesado` with and without `un poco`.** S06's `mistake` claims that dropping `un poco` turns a
    tease into a verdict and makes the line unsayable to the person's face. Confirm the hedge does
    that much work.

96. **The `es broma` timing claim.** S07 is the module's central bet: that `es broma` belongs inside
    the same turn as the tease and that leaving it out lands an insult. Confirm the timing, and
    confirm that `era broma` is for a joke that has already gone wrong rather than simply for the
    past.

97. **`guay` and `mola` in 2026.** Both are shipped as ordinary rather than dated. Confirm neither
    marks the speaker as belonging to a particular generation, which is the one thing the brief's
    note 4 forbids this module.

98. **`hace gracia` built like `gustar`.** S08's `trap` and `mistake` both rest on the flip. Confirm
    `me hace gracia` is the everyday form and that `yo hago gracia` reads as claiming to be the
    funny thing rather than as an error a native would simply not parse.

99. **`chiste` against `broma`.** S08 and S10 split them: the told joke with a punchline versus the
    joke played on somebody, "not interchangeable in either direction". Confirm the split is that
    sharp, and that `una broma pesada` cannot be `un chiste pesado`.

100. **`no me digas` as an invitation to continue.** S09's `trap` says the literal reading (stop
     talking) is the opposite of the used meaning. Confirm, and confirm that it looks enough like
     L4-M1's negative commands (`no hables`, `no pongas`) to be worth the warning.

101. **`qué morro` as half-admiring.** S10's note says the phrase admires the nerve as much as it
     complains about it, and its `usage` says that without a smile it is a real accusation. Confirm
     the double reading, and say whether `qué morro` is regionally narrower than the rest of this
     module.

102. **The tone floor of the whole module.** Every line here is chipped `informal` and rule 6 says
     none of it is safe with a stranger. Confirm there is no line in the ten that a native would
     call harsher than a tease — in particular S07's `¡Qué móvil más feo tienes!` and S10's
     `Qué morro tienes` — and name any that should be softened before the module ships to a
     learner who cannot yet read a room.

### L5-M3 "How they say it there" — two plurals in Spain, one in America, and the ending follows the pronoun

Ten displays:

1. `¿Vosotros habláis español? En México: ¿Ustedes hablan español?`
2. `¿Vosotros sois amigos? Allí dicen: ¿Ustedes son amigos?`
3. `¿Vuestra casa está aquí? Os veo mañana en vuestro trabajo.`
4. `Vos sos mi amigo y vos tenés razón, che.`
5. `Vos podés hablar así allí, y tú puedes hablar así aquí.`
6. `Necesito un coche nuevo aquí; allí necesito un carro nuevo.`
7. `Mi ordenador y mi móvil aquí son mi computadora y mi celular allí.`
8. `¿Queréis un zumo? Acá dicen: ¿Ustedes quieren un jugo?`
9. `Si hablase o tuviese que hablar así, sería otro español.`
10. `¿Vosotros vais a hablar como aquí o como allí? Es vuestro español.`

Twenty-five word rows and seven module rules, and the spine is the brief's note 2: the second
person plural, in both of its systems. Rules 0 and 1 carry the vosotros/ustedes split and its
endings; rule 2 carries voseo; rule 3 the lexical pairs; rule 4 the `-ase` twin of `-ara`; rule 5
is the interference rule (English has one `you`, and an English speaker hears an unfamiliar variant
as an error and corrects it); rule 6 is the module's law, **comprehension of both and production of
one**, plus the statement the brief's note 1 demands — that the Spanish taught by the forty modules
below is a neutral one nobody speaks at home.

Six of the ten displays are the pattern-5 frame, the same utterance said twice in the two varieties
(S01, S02, S06, S07, S08 and, across the two second persons, S05). The paradigm is spread rather
than tabled: `habláis`, `queréis` and `vais` each carry their `ustedes` twin in their own `forms`
list, `sois` stands alone because `son` is L1-M2's, and `dicen` carries `decís`, which is both the
vosotros form and the voseo one. `os` and `vuestro` arrive together in S03 because vosotros is not
one word but a kit. Voseo gets S04 and S05 and its own imperative (`tené` in `tenés`'s forms), and
S05 is the module's argument in one line: `Vos podés hablar así allí, y tú puedes hablar así aquí`.
S09 is the only non-present sentence, and it does exactly what L4-M3 deferred: `hablase` and
`tuviese` beside a conditional L3-M4 already owns. S10 asks the question out loud and hands the
choice back.

No accent is taught anywhere. `sound` on S08 and S09 describes seseo and the peninsular `c`/`z`
(`zumo` is `THU-mo` or `SU-mo`) and says in the same breath that rung does not test it, which is
the brief's note 4. Nothing is slang — `chaval`, `pibe` and `chévere` were all free and all left
free, because M2 owns the durable end of that and note 4 forbids the dating end. Nothing ranks the
varieties: `allí` and `acá` do the naming, so no country is ever the marked one.

### L5-M4 "Formal occasions" — a wish with its head cut off

Ten displays:

1. `Que aproveche. Gracias, igualmente, y gracias por venir hoy.`
2. `Que tenga suerte mañana, y que todo le vaya muy bien.`
3. `Brindemos por los novios y por esta boda. Chinchín.`
4. `Quisiera decir unas palabras en nombre de mi familia.`
5. `Gracias a todos por venir hoy. Es un honor para mí.`
6. `Le acompaño en el sentimiento. Mi más sentido pésame, de verdad.`
7. `Era un hombre muy bueno y muy amable. Que en paz descanse.`
8. `Enhorabuena por la boda, y felicidades a los dos novios.`
9. `Le agradezco mucho este discurso. Muchas gracias a todos ustedes.`
10. `Que descanse usted. Muchas gracias por todo, y hasta mañana.`

Twenty-three word rows and eight rules, and the grammar is one thing: the **independent
subjunctive**, `que` plus a subjunctive with no main clause in front of it. Rule 0 states it, rule 1
names the half that is missing (`deseo que`) — which is the brief's note 3 turned into the teaching
rather than into a warning — and rule 5 says out loud that a learner is right to hear these as
fragments, because they are. Rule 2 is the exhortative (`brindemos`), rule 3 places `ojalá`
(L4-M3's) as the same job with a borrowed word in front of it, rule 4 is the condolence
interference rule, rule 6 splits `enhorabuena` from `felicidades`, and rule 7 pins the register.

Four occasions, four displays each carrying the grammar that generates the next one: the table
(S01, S02, S10), the toast (S03), the speech (S04, S05, S09) and the funeral (S06, S07). `descanse`
appears twice on purpose and as two different rows — `en paz descanse` in S07 and bare `descanse` in
S10 — so that the same verb reaches the learner once as a funeral and once as a good night, and
S10's `trap` says that the three words in the middle are the whole difference.

The module has **no informal item**, as the brief requires: every one of the ten is chipped
`formal`. Nothing is liturgical, nothing is legal, nothing is the announcement register (L4-M7's),
and S07's row note says in as many words that `que en paz descanse` is said by people of every
conviction and carries no belief with it. The one item that could have become a phrasebook entry,
S06, instead ships the interference rule with it: `Lo siento mucho por su padre` is the `mistake`,
and the `why` says it is understood and unmistakably foreign.

### L5-M5 "Big questions" — two subjects, one subjunctive

Ten displays:

1. `Lo importante es que la gente escuche, no que tenga razón.`
2. `Me alegro mucho de que vengas hoy a mi casa.`
3. `Me alegro de que haya venido usted a la boda.`
4. `Me molestó mucho que dijeran eso de mi hermana ayer.`
5. `En el fondo, la gente quiere libertad, justicia y respeto, nada más.`
6. `Lo difícil no es hablar; lo difícil es escuchar de verdad.`
7. `Es una pena que no vinieran a la boda de mi hermana.`
8. `Me alegro de escuchar, y me alegro de que tú escuches también.`
9. `Lo bueno es que hoy el mundo puede hablar de estos valores.`
10. `¿Tú crees que la vida tiene sentido? En el fondo, creo que sí.`

Twenty-two word rows and eight rules. The three systems the brief's note 2 names are rules 0, 1, 2
and 3: the subjunctive of emotion and value with a personal subject; the minimal pair, that the
mood only appears when the two halves have **different subjects**; the sequence of tenses, where the
trigger and not the speaker chooses the tense; and `lo` plus an adjective as an abstract noun.
Rules 4 and 5 are the two interference rules — English is indicative under every one of these
triggers, and English has no `lo`, so a learner builds `la cosa importante` and is understood and
permanently marked. Rule 6 is the register law arriving for the third and last time: state the
claim flat and hedge the frame.

S08 is the module in one sentence — `Me alegro de escuchar, y me alegro de que tú escuches también`
— and it is the only display where both halves of the minimal pair stand side by side with nothing
else changing. The sequence of tenses is spread across three displays that share one trigger family:
present trigger with present subjunctive (S02), present trigger with the perfect subjunctive because
the thing is done (S03, `haya venido`), past trigger with the imperfect (S04, `dijeran`), and a
present trigger over a finished clause (S07, `vinieran`) so that "present trigger" and "present
subjunctive" are never allowed to look like the same rule.

The boldest thing in the module is S09's `trap`, which draws the line the brief does not: `lo
importante es que` demands and takes the subjunctive, `lo bueno es que` reports and does not. That
is open question 122 below. The relative subjunctive of the unknown antecedent (M8's), doubt
(L4-M5's) and concession (L4-M4's) are all left alone, and S10's `trap` says why `crees que` is
indicative and where the negative that flips it lives. No topic is argued: rule 7 says the machinery
is here and the belief is the learner's, and the `usage` lines on S08 and S10 say plainly that none
of this is small talk.

### Brief seams corrected, and what `content:owner` actually said

**1. The single largest correction of this wave: the `ustedes` half was not owned.** L5-M3's brief,
note 2, says the vosotros endings sit "against the third-person plural forms the learner already
owns for ustedes." They do not. `content:owner` on 2026-09-08:

```
hablan	free      tienen	free      quieren	free      dicen	free
van	free      pueden	free      hacen	free      vienen	free
viven	free      saben	free
son	L1-M2     están	L1-M2     ven	L4-M1
```

Three third-person plurals in the whole course, and two of them are `ser` and `estar` from L1-M2.
The consequence is structural: every regional pair in this module had to BUY both halves, not point
one of them at an owned row. That is why `habláis` ships `forms: ["habláis", "hablan"]`, `queréis`
ships `["queréis", "quieren"]` and `vais` ships `["vais", "van"]` — one row per cell of the verb,
carrying both systems — rather than the brief's implied one-row-per-Spain-form. `sois` is the
exception and stands alone precisely because `son` really is L1-M2's.

**2. L5-M3's seam list is otherwise exact.** All thirty-four surfaces it names came back `free`:
`vosotros vosotras os vuestro vuestra ustedes sois tenéis habláis queréis estáis vais hacéis podéis
vos tenés sos che carro ordenador computadora celular zumo jugo patata papa guagua camión chaval
pibe chévere acá hablase tuviese`. So did the four pronouns its pro-drop finding rests on:
`tú free`, `yo free`, `nosotros free`, `ellos free`. And every owned surface it names is owned
exactly where it says: `coche L4-M2`, `móvil L4-M1`, `autobús L2-M4`, `vale L2-M6`, `aquí L1-M7`,
`allí L4-M8`, `pues L4-M5`, `usted L1-M2`, `él L4-M10`, `ella L4-M10`. This is the best-verified
brief seam of the three.

**3. The pro-drop finding is real, and this module spent less of it than the brief expected.** The
brief says the module "BUYS tú and vos and vosotros, and M10 re-uses those rows for its pivot." It
does buy those three. It did **not** buy `yo`, `nosotros` or `ellos`, all still `free` after this
wave: the 25-row cap went on the paradigm instead, and S09 was rewritten from `Si yo hablase…` to
`Si hablase…` to avoid spending a row on a pronoun Spanish drops anyway. M10 inherits `tú`, `vos`
and `vosotros` and must buy the rest itself.

**4. A four-token formula cannot be a key, and two briefs name one.** L5-M4's seam says
`que en paz descanse` is free — it is, but `surfaceSpan` puts it at four tokens, and `check-shown`'s
`own()` does `maxSpan = Math.max(maxSpan, surfaceSpan(key))`, so shipping it as a row would have
silently re-spanned the whole en-es course from 3 to 4 against that brief's own "maxSpan stays 3".
The row shipped is the three-token `en paz descanse`, and the leading `que` resolves to `L2-M5`.
The same trap is in L5-M5's seam list, which names `me alegro de que` (four tokens): the row shipped
is the two-token `me alegro`, with `de → L1-M1` and `que → L2-M5`. Both are the `lo que pasa es que`
precedent the M4 brief itself cites, applied one step earlier than it says.

**5. L5-M4's seam list is exact, and cheaper than it says.** All thirty-four surfaces it names came
back `free`, including `quisiera` (which it flags as listed-free in L4-M3's brief and shipped
unspent — confirmed, still free) and `salud`. Three surfaces it does not name make the module
cheaper than the brief's "most expensive in the level" warning:

```
tenga	L3-M3     vaya	L3-M3     venido	L4-M3     muchas gracias	L2-M1
```

`Que tenga suerte` and `Que todo le vaya muy bien` therefore cost exactly **one** row between them
(`suerte`), not three, and S09's `Muchas gracias` resolves as L2-M1's existing two-token key while
bare `muchas` stays free. The module came in at 23 rows, two under the cap.

**6. `salud` was left alone, by the brief's own second option.** The brief offers two ways out —
gloss both senses in one row, or toast with `chinchín`. The module toasts with `chinchín`, so no
toast owns the noun *health* for the rest of the course and L3-M7's territory is untouched.
`salud` is still `free`.

**7. L5-M5's seam list is exact — and it missed the one that mattered.** Everything it names checks
out: `me molesta L3-M6`, `me parece L3-M3`, `verdad L3-M3`, `sea L3-M3`, `vengas L4-M4`,
`lo L2-M5`, `lo que pasa L4-M4`, `gente L3-M10`, against `me molestó free`, `la gente free`,
`lo que free`, `haya free`. What it does not name is the subjunctive of `hablar`:

```
hable	L4-M6     hables	L4-M6
```

The first draft of S08 built the minimal pair on `hables`, and `npm run content:shown` returned
`RE-TEACH L5-M5-S08 "hables": L4-M6 owns the key, so its note is what a learner is shown`. The row
was replaced with `escuches` (`free`), which is better content as well as a legal row: it ties S08
back to S01's `escuche` and S06's `escuchar`, so one verb carries the whole paradigm across the
module. **This is the wave's argument for running `content:shown` before believing a seam list:
`hable` was easy to check and `hables` was not on anyone's list.**

**8. The `lo` + adjective wholes were already reachable, and are shipped whole anyway.**
`lo → L2-M5`, and `importante → L4-M5`, `bueno → L1-M10`, `difícil → L3-M3` are all owned, so
`lo importante`, `lo bueno` and `lo difícil` would each have resolved as two owned tokens with no
row at all. They are shipped as two-token wholes because `matchSurfaces` walks longest-first: the
learner tapping the phrase must land on the abstraction, not on L2-M5's object pronoun. The brief's
"indexes WHOLE at two tokens and L2-M5's object pronoun keeps its row untouched" is therefore
correct in effect but not for the reason it gives — nothing was blocking the tokens; the whole key
is what wins the tap. Same trick, same reason, for `la gente` (`gente L3-M10`) and `el mundo`.

**9. `estar` is free after forty-two modules.** L1-M2 taught `está`, `estás` and `están`, and no
module has ever written the infinitive: `estar → free`. It was not spent here — an early draft of
S08 needed it and was rewritten — and it is recorded so a later module does not assume it.

**10. Nothing new was learned from a grep.** Every claim in this section is a `content:owner` line.
The two surfaces this wave would have got wrong by scanning — `hables`, and the whole third-person
plural — are both cases where the shipped form differs from the form an author would think to
search for.

### Open questions for the native pass

Continuing the chain: `docs/104` and this file run to 102, so this wave numbers from 103.

103. **The neutral-Spanish claim itself.** M3 rule 6 tells the learner that the Spanish of the forty
     modules below is "a neutral one that nobody speaks at home." Confirm a native reader finds that
     honest rather than dismissive of the course they have just climbed, and that it does not read
     as an apology.

104. **`che` as warm, never rude, and useless outside the region.** S04's note. Confirm the second
     half — that a Spaniard hearing `che` reads it as Argentine rather than as anything else — and
     that a learner using it outside the Río de la Plata sounds like an imitation rather than an
     offence.

105. **`tené` in `tenés`'s forms list.** S04 ships the voseo imperative alongside the present. Confirm
     `tené` is the form actually said (not `tenete`, not `tenlo`) and that shipping it in the same
     row as `tenés`, with no display of its own, is the right density for a learner meeting voseo
     for the first time.

106. **`podés` and `puedes` described as one syllable of difference.** S05's `sound` says
     `po-DES` against `PWE-des`. Confirm that is the whole of it for a listener, and that no vowel
     quality changes that the description misses.

107. **`auto` named but not taught.** S06's note says Argentina and Chile say `auto` while the row
     itself teaches only `carro`. Confirm that mentioning a third word in a note without a row is
     useful rather than confusing, and that `auto` is the right third word rather than `automóvil`.

108. **`computadora` against `computador`.** S07's note gives the feminine to most of America and
     the masculine to Chile and Colombia. Confirm the split and confirm that `ordenador` genuinely
     is not used in America at all, which is what the note claims.

109. **`zumo` heard in America.** S08's note says `zumo` there reads as bookish or foreign rather
     than wrong. Confirm the strength of that: is it noticed, corrected, or simply unremarkable?

110. **`acá` as the ordinary word, with "vaguer edges".** S08's `trap` claims `acá` is not a stronger
     `aquí` but simply the American default. Confirm, and confirm the vagueness claim, which is the
     part most likely to be a grammarian's story rather than a speaker's experience.

111. **`-ase` as habitat, not meaning.** S09's `usage` says a learner should recognise `-ase`
     everywhere and may produce `-ara` for life without anybody minding. Confirm that is true in
     Spain as well as in America, and that no register exists where `-ara` would be heard as wrong.

112. **The `vayáis` aside.** S10's `trap` warns that `vais` and `van` are indicative and that
     `vayáis` is the subjunctive belonging elsewhere. Confirm that naming a form the module does not
     teach helps more than it distracts at this point in the ladder.

113. **`Que aproveche` as obligatory rather than optional.** M4-S01's `usage` says walking past
     somebody eating without saying it "is noticed in Spain." Confirm the strength, and confirm
     whether the same holds in Latin America, where the module implies it does.

114. **`chinchín` against `salud` at a real toast.** M4-S03. The module toasts with `chinchín` to
     leave `salud` free (seam 6). Confirm `chinchín` is what is actually said at a wedding rather
     than a children's word, and say whether the trade is worth it.

115. **`Quisiera` as the standard opening of a short speech.** M4-S04's `usage` calls it "the Spanish
     equivalent of tapping a glass." Confirm, and confirm that `quiero decir unas palabras` really
     does land as blunt rather than merely plain.

116. **`Es un honor` as unremarkable.** M4-S05's note claims it is "not grand in Spanish the way it
     sounds in English" and is ordinary at any small ceremony. Confirm.

117. **The condolence silence.** M4-S06's `trap` says nothing personal is expected and nothing should
     be added — formula, handshake, silence. This is the module's largest cultural claim and the one
     a learner is most likely to override. Confirm it for Spain and separately for Latin America.

118. **`enhorabuena` / `felicidades` and the wedding.** M4-S08 and rule 6 split them earned/arrived
     and then say a wedding takes either. Confirm the split holds outside Spain — several varieties
     are said to prefer `felicidades` across the board — and correct the rule if so.

119. **`agradecer` with no `por`.** M4-S09's `mistake` calls `le agradezco por este discurso` the
     commonest English-speaker error with this verb. Confirm it is genuinely wrong rather than
     regionally acceptable, since `agradecer por` is heard in parts of America.

120. **`Que descanse` as an ordinary evening goodbye.** M4-S10's `usage` says it is the normal way to
     leave a colleague or host at the end of an evening, warmer than `adiós`. Confirm it does not
     read as odd to somebody who is not visibly tired.

121. **`la gente` and singular agreement.** M5-S01's `trap` insists every verb hanging off `la gente`
     is singular. Confirm that a native would never say `la gente escuchen` in speech, and that the
     module is right to state it as absolute.

122. **`lo bueno es que` indicative against `lo importante es que` subjunctive.** M5-S09's `trap` is
     the boldest claim in the wave: that the first reports and takes the indicative while the second
     demands and takes the subjunctive. Confirm the line falls exactly there, and whether
     `lo bueno es que` ever takes the subjunctive in real use.

123. **The obligatory `de` in `me alegro de que`.** M5-S02's note calls dropping it the second
     commonest error. Confirm — and confirm that the note should not also name *dequeísmo*, the
     opposite error, which the module currently leaves out on purpose.

124. **`sentido` as one word doing two jobs.** M5-S10's note ties `tener sentido` (to make sense) to
     M4's `sentido pésame` (felt). Confirm a native reads those as the same word rather than as a
     pun the course invented, since the whole row rests on it.
