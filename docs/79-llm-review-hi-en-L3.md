# hi-en L3 — LLM review

The review that clears each hi-en L3 wave to ship, written in the same change that authors it
(`CLAUDE.md`, "Ship `verified: true` in the authoring change"). The **native-speaker gate is a
separate, stricter bar and stays unmet**: every section below ends in open questions for a native
pass, and no later wave may close one of them by rewriting a shipped module.

Open questions are numbered as a fresh hi-en L3 chain from 1.

## Wave 1 — L3-M1, L3-M2 (#473)

Authored against the briefs written by #464 and the decisions recorded in `docs/71`. A strict
`npm run build` emits `hi-en: 22 modules (L1-M1..M10, L2-M1..M10, L3-M1..M2)` and `CONTENT 202/202
ok`.

The level opens on the asymmetry `docs/71` §1 restates, and both modules lean on it harder than any
L2 module did: **hi-en is the one course whose TARGET language is English**, so its deltas are not
what English has but what English FORBIDS where Hindi allows it. That inverts what a mistake plate
is for. In every other course a plate shows a learner reaching for an English shape the target
language does not have; here it shows a sentence that is correct Hindi thinking, spelled in English
words, which is why all eight plates across this pair are sentences a Hindi speaker actually
produces rather than sentences an author had to invent.

### L3-M1 "Your day, in detail" — the module that opens no row for the three words it is about

Adverb position is the module's grammar and the rule is rigid where Hindi's is free: a frequency
adverb sits immediately before the main verb and immediately after `be`. Hindi's हमेशा can sit
anywhere in the sentence, so there is nothing to transfer and only a seat to learn. But `always`,
`usually` and `never` are all L1-M4's rows, and **a level never edits a file below it**, so the
module that teaches their position opens a row for none of them. The teaching lives in rule 2, in
S03's trap — which puts both chairs in one sentence, `I usually leave the office at seven. I am
never late.` — and in the plates `I drink always tea in the morning` and `I never am late`. Rows
are opened only for what L1-M4 never took: `sometimes`, `rarely`, `hardly ever`. That is the
ratchet's discipline stated positively rather than as a constraint: a spent key is not a lost
lesson, only a lesson that has to live in prose a learner reads instead of a note they would have
to tap.

The delta the module is chartered on is **stative verbs never take `-ing`**, and the list is short
and closed — `know`, `want`, `like`, `need`, `understand`, `mean`, `believe`, `belong`, `own`,
`prefer`. जान रहा हूँ and चाह रहा हूँ are ordinary Hindi, so `*I am knowing` is not carelessness;
it is a correct instinct arriving in the wrong language. The half of the rule that usually goes
missing is the negative: a stative sentence negates with `do not`, not `am not`, so
`*I am not understanding what you mean` needs two repairs and a learner who is told only about
`-ing` makes the second mistake again. S05 spends its trap on exactly that.

`have` is the exception and it gets three sentences rather than a footnote, because it is the one
place the rule has to bend. Possession is stative (`I have a car`, `I have a headache`); an
activity is not (`I am having lunch`, `I am having a bath`). S06 puts both readings of the habit
into a single item — `I have lunch at home every day. Today I am having lunch at work.` — which is
the module's best sentence: the same verb, two forms, both correct, and the difference visible
without a rule being restated. S07's plate, `*I am having a headache today`, is the single most
produced sentence of this level, and S08's trap protects the learner from over-correcting into the
opposite error by saying plainly that `I am having a bath` is right.

`*First I am taking bath` does two jobs in four words: the article-less `take bath` collocation,
and the continuous used for a habit. Both are repaired by one display, `First I have a bath`.

The double-negative ban closes the module. `never`, `rarely` and `hardly ever` carry their own
negation, so `*I do not hardly ever work at night` is one negative too many. It is worth noting
that the same wave authored en-ru L3-M1, whose rule 2 is that Russian **requires** the second
negative — `Ya nikogdá ne p'yu kófe`. Two courses, one wave, and the laws are exact mirrors; a
reviewer reading both should read the two rules together.

### L3-M2 "Work and study" — the article, a closed list, and two verbs with no rule between them

The article before a job is the module's first delta and has no Hindi equivalent at all: मैं
इंजीनियर हूँ has nothing in front of the profession, so the article is dropped every time and the
sentence lands as a headline rather than as speech. `*I am engineer` is the plate, and the a/an
choice is taught by sound rather than by spelling — `a manager`, `an engineer` — which is the only
statement of that rule that survives contact with `an hour` and `a university`.

Uncountable nouns are the module's real subject and `news` is the trap inside the trap: it looks
plural, takes a singular verb, and has no singular of its own. S04 puts it beside `homework` in one
item so the two failure modes sit together — one word that may never take `-s`, and one that
already has an `-s` which counts nothing. The plate is `*The news are not good`.

`do` against `make` is the honest lesson: there is no rule, only two short lists, and करना covers
both, which is why `*Today I did a mistake at work` is the most recognisable slip an Indian English
speaker makes. The module teaches the lists as lists — `do` work, homework, business, a job; `make`
a mistake, a decision, money, a phone call — and then does something better than listing them:
S06 and S07 are `make` sentences and S08 immediately follows with `I do a lot of work`, so a
learner meets the split as a contrast inside three consecutive items rather than as a table.

### The seam the brief had stale, and where the closed list went

The brief's INDEX SEAM note claimed the uncountable list was this module's spend. Read against the
REAL cumulative index — the fold of `L1-M1` through `L2-M10`, **513 surfaces, maxSpan 3** —
`information`, `advice`, `furniture`, `luggage`, `piece`, `a piece of` and `a lot of` are **all
L2-M3's**. That module gave its S07–S10 to exactly this lesson: `I need some advice`, `I have got a
lot of luggage`, `Can I have a piece of bread?`. Only `equipment`, `homework` and `news` were
genuinely fresh.

First-occurrence-wins makes a second row a note nobody will ever be shown, so the module could not
re-row the seven, and it may not edit L2-M3 to reach them. The closed list therefore moved into
`rules[1]`, and that is where a list belongs anyway: a rule is read, whereas a row is only reached
by tapping a word, and a learner can only tap the words the module shows. Rows were opened for the
three fresh ones — `equipment` (S05), `homework` and `news` (S04) — and the other five appear in
prose and in displays that L2-M3 already taught. The brief was corrected in place, with the
correction marked as the authoring wave's, so #482 and the M6–M10 wave inherit the real index
rather than the guess.

### The `work` row that was dropped, and why dropping it is the teaching

`work` is L1-M4's row, and the note there reads `काम करना — क्रिया: I work · she works`. True of
the verb; **false of the noun** M2 needs. Both repairs were closed: a second row here would be
unreachable, and editing L1-M4 is forbidden. So a learner tapping `work` inside `a lot of work`
would have been shown a note telling them it is a verb — the wrong fact, delivered at exactly the
moment they were asking.

The teaching moved into S03's trap, which names L1-M4 explicitly: `work` is the same word that was
a verb there, it is a noun here, it is not counted, and if you must count, the word is `job` or the
frame is `a lot of work`. A trap is read with the sentence rather than tapped, so it reaches every
learner who reaches the item — which is precisely what the row could not do. The plate is
`*My manager gives me many works`, and S01's `job` row carries the other half: `job` is the
countable partner of the uncountable `work`.

This is the sharpest instance so far of the rule the ladder has been applying since L1. A level
never edits a file below it, and the repair for a stale note below is prose above, never a second
row.

### `for` is L3-M7's, so a company takes `at`

The brief's pattern list offered `I work + at/for + <company>`, but `for` belongs to M7, which owns
both `for` and `since` so that their notes can be written true of every later job they do. M2
therefore cannot write "I work for a company" at all. S02 is `I work at a bank`, with the trap
reserving `in` for a city, and the module's declared `allowedPatterns` were re-cut to match — a
pattern a module is not permitted to write is a false statement in a shipped file.

Two other patterns moved for the same kind of reason. `I need some + <uncountable>` was dropped
because L2-M3-S07 is literally `I need some advice`, and `The + <uncountable> + is + Adj` took its
place, which is the frame the singular-verb fact actually needs and which no earlier module took.

### The field-language rule this course alone has

`src/course/types.test.ts` holds hi-en's inverted language law (#270), and this wave moved its
module count to 22: **English appears only in the L2 slots** — sentence, word, variation, mistake
and pool `display`, and word `forms` — and **every teaching field is Hindi in Devanagari**. No
sentence carries `glossEn`; every sentence carries `literal`.

It is worth stating in a review because of what it means for a reviewer. Every English display and
every plate quoted above is an L2 slot; every argument under it — the rule texts, the traps, the
notes, each `mistake.why` — is Hindi. The interference claims of this level are claims about
English, made in Hindi, about a Hindi habit. A native pass on this course needs both languages at
once, and a reviewer who reads only the English half will see the plates and none of the reasoning
that makes them teach.

### The ratchet

`tools/shown-surfaces.test.ts` held at **hi-en 30** across both modules, and this wave produced no
finding at all — no shown-but-untaught surface in either file. That is the first wave of the
milestone with none, and the reason is the seam correction above: both modules were authored
against the real index rather than the brief's guess, so every surface they show is one an earlier
module already owns or one they open themselves. No baseline moved in either direction.

### Open questions for the native pass

1. **The stative list** (M1, rule 0). Confirm the ten — `know`, `want`, `like`, `need`,
   `understand`, `mean`, `believe`, `belong`, `own`, `prefer` — are the right closed list for a
   learner at this rung, and that nothing commoner is missing from it.
2. **The stative negative** (M1-S05). Confirm `I do not understand` rather than `I am not
   understanding` is heard as a correction rather than as a stylistic preference, and that a
   listener notices immediately.
3. **`have` split three ways** (M1, rule 1; S06–S08). Confirm `I am having a headache` is genuinely
   wrong rather than merely marked, while `I am having lunch` and `I am having a bath` are
   unremarkable.
4. **`have a bath`** (M1-S08). The module teaches `have a bath` and plates `taking bath`. Confirm
   that `take a bath`, with the article, is not so ordinary in the Englishes this learner will meet
   that banning it outright misleads them.
5. **The two adverb chairs** (M1, rule 2; S03). Confirm `I am never late` and `I never am late`
   split as absolutely as the trap claims, with no emphatic reading that rescues the second.
6. **`sometimes` roams, `rarely` does not** (M1-S09). Confirm `Sometimes I take the train` and
   `I sometimes take the train` are both ordinary, and that fronted `Rarely I take the bus` really
   is odd rather than merely formal.
7. **`hardly ever`** (M1-S10). Confirm it is everyday spoken English rather than bookish, and that
   the negative-concord ban is worth a whole plate at this level.
8. **`leave the office`** (M1-S03). Confirm `I leave from the office` is wrong rather than regional,
   given how widely it is said.
9. **The article before a job** (M2, rule 0; S01). Confirm `I am engineer` is heard at once by an
   English ear, and that "the sound chooses a or an" is a safe rule to give with no exceptions
   named.
10. **The uncountable list** (M2, rule 1). Confirm `work`, `homework`, `information`, `advice`,
    `equipment`, `luggage`, `furniture`, `news` are the eight to teach, and whether `a piece of
    information` is what a speaker actually says or a textbook frame a learner should recognise
    rather than produce.
11. **`news` singular** (M2, rule 2; S04). Confirm `The news are` occurs in no standard variety, and
    that `a news` is impossible rather than merely rare.
12. **The `do`/`make` lists** (M2, rule 3; S06–S08). Confirm the two fours are the right members,
    and that `make money` and `make a phone call` are the everyday collocations rather than
    `earn money` and `phone someone`.
13. **`I work at a bank`** (M2-S02). `for` is unavailable until M7, so the module teaches `at` for a
    workplace and reserves `in` for a city. Confirm a learner restricted to `at` for the next five
    modules never sounds odd naming an employer.
