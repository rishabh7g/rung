### 1.2 The sayings-orthography decision, executed and read back

`docs/133` §2 settled it by running `surface.ts`: `ālea` and `alea` are two keys, so the course writes
one. Read back from the folded index:

```
alea present: False    ālea present: True
```

M1-S03 writes `Ālea iacta est` and its trap says out loud that every mug and film poster says `alea`,
that they are the same word, and that they would be two rows here. That is the third time this course
has taken that shape of decision — after `ēsse` (L2-M5) and `SPQR` (L4-M7) — and the first time the
familiar spelling is the one being refused.

Each saying carries its source register in the usage line: Horace for `carpe diem`, Suetonius quoting
Augustus for `festīnā lentē`, Caesar as Suetonius reports him for `ālea iacta est`, and "proverbial,
and late" for `in vīnō vēritās`. `requiēscat in pāce` is written nowhere — it is M4's.

### 1.3 The module whose content the learner already had

M1's first sentence is `Carpe diem`, and its trap is the only one in the course that says the learner
has known the sentence for years and can read it for the first time now. `carpe` is L2-M5's imperative
shape and `diem` is its object, and the plate is `Carpe diēs` — the subject shape as an object, which
is "the sort of thing a learner never notices until they can parse it".

`ālea iacta est` turns out to be **L2-M8's arrangement thirty-eight rungs later**: a participle used as
an adjective with `sum`, agreeing with what it describes. So the most famous participle in the language
is an exercise in L2-M3's agreement rule, and its plate is the masculine ending on a feminine noun.

### 1.4 `ut` gets its second reading, and the remedy is L4-M6's for the third time

M1-S05 needs `ut dīcitur` ("as they say"), and `ut`'s key has been L3-M4's purpose reading for
thirty-eight modules. Withholding was not available — the frame is what makes a saying quotable — so
the rows name **both** readings and state the signal: **an indicative after `ut` means "as", a
subjunctive means purpose.** The plate is `ut dīcātur`, which is grammatical and a different sentence.

That is the same remedy L4-M6's `cum` rows use, now on its third outing, and `docs/133` §7 records the
refusal condition it comes with: if a module cannot afford the note, the phrase is not worth the key.

### 1.5 The vocative arrives, forty-nine rungs after L1 got away without one

L2-M7 wanted `Mārce` for a telephone call and could not have it; `docs/127` §4 named the gap and every
level since repeated it. M2 is its owner, and **mock address is why**: L1 got away without a vocative
because every name it greeted — `Iūlia`, `magister`, `discipula` — has an address shape identical to its
nominative, and `Mārcus` does not.

The cost is visible in the plates: **three of M2's ten are the subject shape used to address somebody**
(`Ō Mārcus`, `Ō stultus` twice, at the front of a sentence and at the end). That is what a shape
deferred for forty-nine modules costs when it lands, and the module writes the vocative at both ends of
its sentence because the **end** is the commoner position.

### 1.6 Humour stayed at the Plautine end, and the register is in the usage lines

`docs/133` §3 settled it. M2 teases and does not humiliate: `ō stulte` between friends, `nimium bibistī`
the morning after, and S09's `sed nōn rīdēbis` — a threat which is entirely **one tense**, L1-M6's
future forty-four rungs on, and which the trap says is "as close as this module comes to Martial and it
stops there deliberately".

The sarcasm costs nothing: `optimē` has been in the learner's mouth since L1-M2 as the answer to
`quid agis?`, and said over a broken table it reverses. The module's rule states why that needs help —
**Latin's words carry no tone of their own**, so irony here needs the contradiction supplied, which is
what S06's colon does.

And `quid agis?` is the module's neatest find: L1-M2's greeting becomes an accusation when `tandem` is
added, which shows how little of tone lives in vocabulary.

### 1.7 M3's mechanism held: `display` never moved

`docs/133` §5's whole decision was that one sentence gets one spelling and `sound` carries the variants.
Executed: every `sound` line in M3 names what a restored-classical, an ecclesiastical and (once) an
English-school reader does with the letters on the page, and **not one display differs**. The index
gained no key for a variant.

The best of them is S10's, which counts the cost: the ecclesiastical tradition "changes eight sounds in
this sentence and not one letter". S08's is the sharpest — `Cicerō` is `KEE-ke-roh` restored,
`CHEE-che-roh` ecclesiastical, `SISS-er-oh` in an English school, and all three are reading six letters.

**The register spectrum is taught by writing the same content twice.** S05 is Plautine (short,
coordinate) and S06 is Ciceronian (a relative clause inside the subject, a pronoun pointing back) — and
nothing in the higher version is new grammar, which is the point: *level* is what marks a learner, not
accent. S07 is the Vulgar drift, `dē` twice where a classical writer needed no preposition at all, and
its trap says nobody wrote it on purpose and everybody drifted towards it.

M3 also keeps **three of its plates on `dē` plus the wrong case**, deliberately: a module whose content
is prose about pronunciation still has to spend its plates on the grammar a learner is getting wrong.

### 1.8 Corrections made during the wave

- **Three modules were written with enrichment blocks missing** — M1–M3 of any level ship fully
  enriched — and thirty-one `sound` lines, `mistake` plates and `mnemonic`s were written rather than
  any module demoted. That is the third level running where this rule has cost real work.
- **One note ran to 203 characters** against a 200 limit and was shortened.
- A variation reached for `scrībis` and `stilum`, neither owned, and was rewritten around taught words
  rather than given rows it did not need.
- Eight shapes were shown before they were owned and each went into its own lexeme's row: `carpō`,
  `hōram`, `āleae`, `iactae`, `horātius`, `festīnō`, `rōmānum`, `pulchra`.

### 1.9 Open questions for the fluent-speaker gate

Additions to the hundred standing in the docs listed across `docs/123`–`docs/133`.

1. **Is refusing `alea` right?** `docs/133` §10 asked it of the brief and M1-S03 now writes the
   macroned form with a trap explaining the other. A teacher may still say a quotation should be spelled
   as received — which would mean the course's orthography does not apply to quoted material, a decision
   with consequences well beyond this module.
2. **Is `ō stulte` affectionate enough to teach?** M2 says between friends it is and to a stranger it is
   not, and a learner who mistakes the situation has insulted somebody. `ō stultissime` would be
   plainly playful and costs a superlative.
3. **Does `quid tandem agis?` read as impatient or as angry?** The module treats `tandem` as
   impatience; a fluent reader may find it stronger than that.
4. **Are three pronunciation traditions teachable in prose with no audio?** `docs/133` §10 asked this of
   the brief. Ten `sound` lines later it reads as the most useful part of the module or the most
   theoretical, and only a reader who says Latin aloud can tell which.
5. **Is S07's double `dē` a fair picture of Vulgar Latin**, or a caricature of a change that took
   centuries?
6. **Is `cīvis Rōmānus sum` too famous a sentence for a module about register?** It is Cicero's own and
   it is three words — which the trap notes is not a Ciceronian period at all, and may undercut the
   module's own spectrum.

### 2.2 The last two constructions the course owed

L5-M6 brings the **ablative absolute** (`hīs rēbus dictīs`, "these things having been said") and
L5-M7 the **indirect question** (`quaerō quid agās`). With those the fifty-module syllabus in `docs/121`
has nothing outstanding: every construction the course promised is now taught somewhere, and the three
remaining modules (#653) owe consolidation rather than new grammar.

Both arrive late on purpose and both are *made of parts the learner already owns*. The ablative
absolute needs only the perfect participle (L2-M8) and the ablative (L2-M4); the indirect question
needs only the present subjunctive (L3-M4) and the question words of L1-M2. Neither module teaches a
new ending. M6's rules say so out loud, because a learner who thinks the ablative absolute is a new
tense will look for a form that does not exist.

### 2.3 `crēdō` landed at M1, not at M5, and that is what M1 said would happen

M5 is the module that teaches `crēdō`'s two constructions — accusative + infinitive for a belief,
dative for a person you trust. The key resolves to **L5-M1**, because M1-S08's `nōn tamen crēdō` wrote
the surface first and **first occurrence wins** in the word index.

This is the `cum` situation of L1-M7 and the `ut` situation of L3-M4 in its mildest form: the collision
was anticipated in the authoring, M1's note already points forward ("M5 of this level teaches its two
constructions"), and M5's rows teach the constructions without needing to own the bare key. No build
gate fires and no learner is misled. It is recorded here because the *pattern* — an early module
spending a key a later module is built around — has now happened four times in this course and is the
single most common way an en-la plan goes wrong.

### 2.4 The vocative's second use, and the one word that is declined instead

L5-M2 taught the vocative as address between friends. M4 gives it its formal use — `avē atque valē`,
`grātiās tibi agō`, `bene tibi` — and then S08 writes `Mārcus mortuus est`, where **`Mārcus` is
nominative**, not vocative, because you do not address the dead. The trap says exactly that. It is the
only place in fifty modules where a name's case carries the whole meaning of the sentence, and the
usage line is doing more work than the rule.

`requiēscat in pāce` is the one row in the course where the **ecclesiastical register** is at home
rather than a mistake. Its usage line says so and says where the boundary is, because a learner who
generalises from it will write church Latin in a classical sentence.

### 2.5 Negation inside a word reached eight

`nēmō` (M5) and `ignōrō` (M7) are the seventh and eighth words in the course whose negative is baked
in. M5 plates `nēmō nōn` as an error; **M6 then uses `nēmō nōn` deliberately** as a rhetorical device,
and its usage line names the M5 plate by what it says rather than by number. That is deliberate and it
is the riskiest thing in the wave: a learner who meets the plate and the device in the wrong order has
been told a thing is wrong and then shown it. §2.8 asks whether the ordering survives a fluent reader.

M7's litotes rows (`nōn male`, `nōn ignōrō`) are the same shape one level up — a double negative as
understatement — and they are the reason M7 rather than M5 owns `ignōrō`.

### 2.6 Corrections made during the wave

- **`vēritāsque` (M5) and `venit-ne` (M4)** — two seams opened in modules that are not in
  `SEAM_MODULES`. Replaced with `et` and with `Agis-ne bene?`, which L1-M2 already owns. The rule is
  that only the five seam modules may join an enclitic, and neither M4 nor M5 is one of them.
- **`requiēscant` (M4), `deum` (M5), `facilis` and `dicta` (M6), `dīcam` (M7)** — surfaces shown with
  no owning row. Each was given a row in its own lexeme or the display was rewritten; none was
  exempted.
- **Enrichment** — `ENRICHMENT_FULL_THROUGH_MODULE = 3` binds only a level's first three modules, so
  none of M4…M7 was required to carry `sound`, `mistake` or `mnemonic` at all. They carry 38 `sound`
  lines, 12 `mistake` plates and 29 `mnemonic`s between them anyway, because at this level the
  difficulty is pronunciation and register rather than form (`avē`, `requiēscat`, `hīs rēbus dictīs`).
- **Two notes over `NOTE_MAX_CHARS`** were shortened rather than split.

### 2.7 Reserved keys still unspent

Read back from the folded index after M7: `sīc`, `rosa`/`rosā`, `possim`, `meus`, `eius`, `vōs`,

Every key the course has reserved across five levels has been spent in the module that promised it and

### 2.8 Open questions for the fluent-speaker gate

Additions to the hundred-odd standing across `docs/123`–`docs/134` §1.9.

1. **Is M6's deliberate `nēmō nōn` teachable one module after M5 plates it as an error?** A fluent
   reader may say the device belongs in a rhetoric lesson and the plate should stand alone.
2. **Is `hīs rēbus dictīs` the right first ablative absolute?** It is the commonest shape in oratory
   and it is also four words of abstraction; a concrete one (`urbe captā`) would be easier to picture
   and harder to reuse.
3. **Does `at` vs `sed` survive contact with real prose?** M6 teaches `at` as the sharper adversative.
   Cicero's usage is looser than the module admits.
4. **Is `immō` "a correction upwards" or simply a correction?** The module claims the upward force; a
   fluent reader may find it neutral.
5. **Is `quod sī` too rare to spend a row on**, given that `quod` already carries two readings and this
   is its third?
6. **Do the four ways of asking for help in M7 rank as the module says** — blunt to elaborate — or is
   the ranking a modern politeness intuition read back into Latin?
7. **Is litotes without audio teachable at all?** `nōn male` is a tone of voice on the page.
