This file records the LLM review of the hi-en fifth level, module by module, in the wave that
authored each one. It is the first L5 review doc for this course, so it opens here rather than
continuing an earlier file; `docs/106-hi-en-L5-brief-decisions.md` is the brief it is written
against, and its numbered open questions are continued rather than restarted.

**The index this wave was written against.** Every ownership claim below was produced by
`npm run content:owner -- hi-en "<surface>" …` on 2026-09-08, and every run in this wave closed
with the same footer:

> `1055 surfaces owned, folded over 40 modules through L4-M10`

So the fold is exactly the one `docs/106` §3 was checked against — 1055 surfaces over forty
modules, L1-M1 through L4-M10, with no L5 module of any course yet emitted. The seam the brief
warned about (`docs/106` §3.1(5): an index seam names a count and a module, and both go stale) had
not moved by the time these two modules were written. That is the only wave in this course for
which that will be true, and the next wave must re-run the check rather than quote this paragraph.

## Wave 1 — L5-M1, L5-M2 (#575)

### L5-M1 "Sayings and idioms" — the fixed phrase is one lexical item wearing several words

The ten displays:

1. `This expression is an idiom. Do not take it literally.`
2. `The exam was a piece of cake.`
3. `She called me out of the blue.`
4. `We go to the market once in a while.`
5. `Now and then I think about my old school.`
6. `Sooner or later he will tell you.`
7. `I know it by heart. I do not need the book.`
8. `He did not do it on purpose. Never mind, it was just bad luck.`
9. `Hang on, I am coming. I am not late.`
10. `I am fed up with this work. Take it easy, he said.`

**What it teaches.** The FIXED PHRASE, in the brief's three testable parts, one per rule. `rules[0]`
is the no-synonym law (`a piece of cake`, never `a slice of cake`); `rules[1]` is the frozen
internal grammar (`once in a time` and `out of the blues` are both broken, and so is
`a very big piece of cake` — an idiom is not made emphatic by lengthening it, which is the brief's
quieter second interference and is carried again by S02's `mistake`); `rules[2]` is the part-of-speech
slot, which is what makes `a piece of cake` a noun phrase after `be`, `once in a while` and
`now and then` adverbials, and `never mind` · `hang on` · `take it easy` whole turns.
`rules[3]` is the Hindi pair the brief asked for: the Hindi idiom, the literal English it produces,
and the English idiom that does the job — `सर मत खाओ` → `do not eat my head`, `दिमाग़ खा गया` →
`he ate my brain`, `बाएँ हाथ का खेल` → `a game of the left hand`, whose real English is
`a piece of cake`. `rules[4]` carries the second half of the job line: these are neutral-to-informal
speech and none belongs in L4-M7's official register, and it is where the two five-token idioms
(`once in a blue moon`, `off the top of my head`) are NAMED without being shown.

S01 is the frame sentence and is deliberately meta: it opens `expression`, `idiom` and `literally`
so that the nine sentences after it have a word for what they are doing. Sentence 8 is the only
three-row sentence, because `never mind` reads best as the answer to `on purpose` and `bad luck`
is the reason it is offered.

The module is `informal` on S02, S08, S09 and S10 and `neutral` elsewhere — the register chip is
doing the "and when it is used" half of the job line at the item level, and `hang on` is the one
whose `trap` bars it from writing and from official language outright.

Fifteen new keys against a cap of 25: eleven whole phrases (`a piece of cake`, `out of the blue`,
`once in a while`, `now and then`, `sooner or later`, `by heart`, `on purpose`, `never mind`,
`hang on`, `fed up`, `take it easy`) and four bare words (`idiom`, `expression`, `literally`,
`luck`), plus the two plurals `idioms` and `expressions` on their own rows' `forms`.

### L5-M2 "Humour and teasing" — English flags a joke with words, and the flags have a fixed place

The ten displays:

1. `I'm only joking. Do not take it the wrong way.`
2. `That was a silly question — just kidding.`
3. `No offence, but this shop is not very good.`
4. `That was a joke. I did not mean it.`
5. `He is very funny, but he is never serious.`
6. `Do not tease him. He does not take a joke.`
7. `Come on, do not take it seriously.`
8. `The food was not bad at all. She was very happy.`
9. `I meant it as a joke. She was offended.`
10. `Think about her feelings. Do not offend her.`

**What it teaches.** The MARKED JOKE. `rules[0]` names the closed set and its two positions —
`no offence, but …` before, `I'm only joking` · `just kidding` after — and says the flag is made of
WORDS, not of delivery. `rules[1]` is the position law itself, and it is the one the module tests
twice: S02's `mistake` is `Just kidding, that was a silly question`, a flag moved to the front,
which stops being a flag and becomes a warning. `rules[2]` is understatement as warmth, which S08
carries: `not bad at all` is praise and the `trap` says so in as many words, because a learner reads
it as lukewarm and answers as though criticised. `rules[3]` is the brief's three interferences —
deadpan delivery, personal remarks, and the seniority licence that `आप`/`तुम` marks and English
cannot — and the personal-remarks half is stated as a fact about the audience, never as a fault in
the learner, in S03's and S10's `trap` and in S06's. `rules[4]` is the split with L5-M7, written
explicitly so the two modules do not blur: this module owns friendly irony that is marked and
immediately defused, L5-M7 owns unmarked implication and sarcasm. `rules[4]` is also where `hurt`
is named without being shown (see the seams below).

The repair is one short fixed line and not an apology, as the brief required: S04's
`I did not mean it` and S09's `I meant it as a joke` are the whole of it, and S04's `trap` says
plainly that `I did not mean it` is not an apology — it only clears up the intention.

Twenty-one new keys against a cap of 25: eight whole phrases (`only joking`, `the wrong way`,
`just kidding`, `no offence`, `mean it`, `take a joke`, `come on`, `not bad at all`) and thirteen
bare surfaces across nine rows (`silly`, `joke`/`jokes`/`joking`, `funny`, `serious`/`seriously`,
`tease`/`teasing`, `meant`, `offended`/`offend`, `feelings`).

**2. `not bad at all` swallows an OWNED key, and the brief's swallow list does not say so.**

> `at all    free`

`not bad at all` is praise. Longest-match-first means the four-token key wins wherever the whole

`trap` tells the learner not to hear the praise as a cold answer. This was found by asking `content:owner` about the phrase's PARTS, not the phrase — the
brief's fresh-and-whole list had already told the truth about the phrase.

### Open questions for the native-speaker gate

Continuing the chain in `docs/106-hi-en-L5-brief-decisions.md`, whose highest number is 119.

120. **`a piece of cake` as the plate for `बाएँ हाथ का खेल`** (M1, `rules[3]`). The rule pairs three
     Hindi idioms with the nonsense their literal English produces and gives the English idiom that
     really does the job. Confirm `a piece of cake` is the phrase an Indian English speaker would
     actually reach for here rather than `child's play`, which is closer to the Hindi vehicle and
     which the module does not teach.

121. **`out of the blue` for a phone call** (M1-S03). Confirm the idiom is neutral about whether the
     surprise was welcome. The Hindi cue is `उसने अचानक ही मुझे फ़ोन कर दिया`, which reads slightly
     pleased; if the English leans negative the cue needs to lean with it.

122. **`once in a while` against `now and then`** (M1-S04, S05). The module teaches them as
     interchangeable adverbials with one Hindi gloss each (`कभी-कभी`, `कभी-कभार`). Confirm there is
     no frequency difference a learner would be heard getting wrong, and that the two Hindi glosses
     are not themselves distinguishable in a way English does not support.

123. **`by heart` glossed as `ज़बानी`** (M1-S07). Confirm `ज़बानी याद` is the everyday Hindi rather
     than `कंठस्थ`, which the `cue` also offers and which may read as bookish to a learner whose
     Hindi is colloquial.

124. **`never mind` as a reply to someone else's mistake** (M1-S08). The module places it after
     `He did not do it on purpose` and the `trap` says it is not an apology. Confirm it does not
     read as dismissive when the mistake was real, and that `कोई बात नहीं` is the right pairing
     rather than `छोड़िए`.

125. **`hang on` barred from writing** (M1-S09, `trap`). The trap says it never appears on paper or
     in official language. Confirm the line is drawn in the right place for Indian English, where
     `hang on` may be more written than the brief assumes.

126. **`fed up with`, and whether `of` is heard** (M1-S10). The module teaches `with` and marks
     `from` as the Hindi `से` showing through. Indian English also has `fed up of`. Confirm whether
     that variant is common enough that the `trap` should name it as a third option to avoid rather
     than leaving it unmentioned.

127. **`take it easy` as a calming line rather than a farewell** (M1-S10). The module teaches only
     the calming sense. Confirm the goodbye sense is rare enough in the learner's English that
     leaving it out is safe, since a learner who meets it as a farewell has no row to reach.

128. **The em dash before `just kidding`** (M2-S02). The display is
     `That was a silly question — just kidding.` Confirm the dash is what a speaker's pause becomes
     in writing, and that a comma would not be the ordinary choice — the module never teaches the
     dash as punctuation, it only shows it.

129. **`no offence, but` and whether the flag actually helps** (M2-S03). The module teaches it as a
     working flag and its `trap` says it does NOT license a personal remark. Confirm English hearers
     accept `no offence, but` on an opinion about a thing (a shop, a piece of work), rather than
     hearing it as a signal that something rude is coming regardless.

130. **`I did not mean it` as intention-clearing and not apology** (M2-S04, `trap`). This is the
     module's whole repair and the brief forbade an apology at length. Confirm a native hearer
     treats the line as sufficient in a light exchange, and that `sorry` is genuinely optional after
     it.

131. **`take a joke` in the negative only** (M2-S06). The module teaches `He does not take a joke`
     and never the positive `He can take a joke`. Confirm the positive is ordinary enough that its
     absence is a gap worth filling in a later wave, or rare enough that the negative alone is right.

132. **`not bad at all` as full praise** (M2-S08). This is the module's most consequential claim and
     the one a learner is likeliest to mis-hear. Confirm it is praise rather than faint approval in
     ordinary speech, and confirm that `thank you` is the expected reply — the `trap` states both.

133. **`offended` against `angry`** (M2-S09, `trap`). The line drawn is ग़ुस्सा against
     अपमान का बुरा मानना. Confirm the distinction survives in the learner's Hindi, where
     `बुरा मान गया` may cover both.

134. **Deadpan delivery stated as a fact about the audience** (M2, `rules[3]`). The rule says an
     English joke is often said flat, and that the Hindi laugh cue reads as trying too hard.
     Confirm this generalisation is fair across the varieties an Indian learner will actually meet,
     rather than true of one of them.

135. **The personal-remarks rule as an absolute** (M2, `rules[3]` and S03, S06, S10 `trap`). Weight,
     skin, marriage and salary are stated as not jokes in English at ANY level of friendship.
     Confirm the absolute is the right teaching even where it is not quite the truth — the brief
     called this the module's most useful single sentence, and softening it would cost the learner
     the protection it is there to give.

### L5-M3 "How they say it there" — a variety is a place and a time, not a grade

The ten displays:

1. `Have you ever heard the word lift? In America they say elevator.`
2. `A flat in Britain is an apartment in America.`
3. `My grandmother's generation says film, and my generation says movie.`
4. `That word is slang. My grandmother never says it.`
5. `Have you ever been to Britain? I have never been there.`
6. `The British spelling is colour. The American spelling is color.`
7. `The same word is realise in Britain and realize in America.`
8. `That sounds very formal. This word is more informal.`
9. `Indian English is local English, and it is not wrong.`
10. `Do not mix the two spellings. That sounds wrong to an outsider.`

**What it teaches.** Two systems, as `docs/106` §3.3(2) asked. The first is VARIETY AS A CHOICE:
`rules[0]` is the premise — English is a family of forms exactly as Delhi, Bhopal and Patna Hindi
are, and the learner's English is one of them rather than a damaged copy of someone else's;
`rules[2]` is the machinery, the five pairs (`lift`/`elevator`, `flat`/`apartment`, `film`/`movie`,
`colour`/`color`, `realise`/`realize`) and the one rule that is actually testable, CONSISTENCY.
S10 carries that rule as its own sentence and its `trap` states the counter-intuitive half: what
marks an outsider is not choosing the "wrong" form but mixing two.

The second system is the debt `docs/88` §5 deferred out of L4 with no owner named: the EXPERIENCE
USE OF THE PRESENT PERFECT. `rules[1]` states it with the constraint that makes it teachable — no
time expression at all, and a finished time (`yesterday`, `in 2019`) forces the past simple. Both
S01 and S05 carry it, and both `mistake` rows are the same error twice, once with `yesterday` and
once with `in 2019`, because that is the one thing a learner does with this form.

`rules[3]` is the register interference the brief asked for: Hindi grammaticalises register on
तू / तुम / आप and English has one `you`, so the learner concludes English has no register at all —
S08's `formal`/`informal` pair is where that lands, and the rule points back at L2-M1's politeness
scale rather than restating it. `rules[4]` is `docs/88` §1's standing rule kept: Indian English is
named as a full variety, MARKED outside India rather than wrong, with the official register
(`kindly`, `do the needful`, `prepone`) left where L4-M7 has it. That rule is also where `accent`
and `dialect` are named — in Hindi, in `rules[].text` only. This course has no audio
(`docs/01-plan.md`), so an accent is described and never asked for, which is why neither word
appears in a display and neither costs a key.

Twenty-two rows against a cap of 25: two whole keys (`have you ever`, `have never`) and twenty bare
words, six of which carry a second surface on their own row's `forms` — `Britain`/`British`,
`America`/`American`, `realize`/`realise`, `heard`/`hear`, `word`/`words`, `spelling`/`spellings`.
The module is `informal` on S04 alone (`slang` is the only item that is itself informal) and
`neutral` elsewhere.

### L5-M4 "Formal occasions" — the ceremonial register is short, and that is the whole delta

The ten displays:

1. `I would like to say a few words about my teacher.`
2. `We are here today to celebrate a wedding.`
3. `Please join me in a toast. Raise a glass to the couple.`
4. `I'm so sorry for your loss.`
5. `We are here today to remember a good teacher.`
6. `This speech is in memory of my grandmother.`
7. `Congratulations! Our best wishes for the wedding.`
8. `My condolences to you and your family.`
9. `It is an honour to speak on this occasion.`
10. `On behalf of the family, thank you all. We are grateful.`

**What it teaches.** This is the production half of the debt L4-M7 named, and it is production from
the first sentence. `rules[0]` is the CEREMONIAL FRAME in three fixed parts — an opening
(`On behalf of …`, `I would like to say a few words`, `We are here today to …`), a body in `we` and
`our` with no contractions, and a closing (`Please join me in …`, `In memory of …`,
`Thank you all for coming`). S02 and S05 are deliberately the same frame twice with one verb
changed, `celebrate` against `remember`, because that IS the teaching: one opening serves a wedding
and a memorial, and only the verb moves.

`rules[1]` is the delta and the reason the module's `maxWordsPerSentence` goes DOWN to 12 rather
than up: ceremonial English is SHORT and PLAIN. The longest display is twelve words and the
condolence is six. `rules[2]` is the interference that produces the marked official register —
Hindi's ceremonial register reaches for Sanskritised vocabulary and long balanced sentences, so the
learner reaches for Latinate English and writes
*`It is my privilege to avail this opportunity to convey my heartiest felicitations`*, quoted in the
rule as the thing not to write, against the real English of a wedding speech. `rules[3]` is the
second interference and it earns its own rule because it is a rule about STOPPING: an English
condolence is formulaic and brief, and S04's `mistake` is the learner continuing —
`I'm so sorry for your loss. What was the illness?` — which in Hindi is warmth and in English is
intrusion.

`rules[4]` is the index fact `docs/106` §3.4 asked for, and it is stated as a reading skill rather
than as a prohibition: official `shall` (`Applicants shall submit two copies`) and optative `may`
(`May you both be very happy`) are named, in Hindi, with a pointer to whose rows they are
(L2-M6's suggestion `shall`, L4-M7's permission `may`), and neither appears in any `display`,
`variation`, `mistake` or pool item in this module.

Nineteen rows against a cap of 25: six whole keys (`on behalf of`, `a few words`, `please join me`,
`sorry for your loss`, `in memory of`, `thank you all`) and eleven bare words. Every sentence is
`formal` — the only module in the level with a single register chip throughout, which is the job
line doing its own work.

### L5-M5 "Big questions" — the third article this course teaches is the absence of one

The ten displays:

1. `Freedom is important, but it is not everything.`
2. `Money is not happiness. Most people know that.`
3. `I believe that truth matters more than money.`
4. `It depends on what you value in life.`
5. `People tend to forget the past.`
6. `In general, life is not fair. That is the truth.`
7. `On the whole, society is better than it was.`
8. `Some people say that duty is more important than happiness.`
9. `Human nature does not change very much.`
10. `Some people value freedom. I value justice and duty.`

**What it teaches.** `rules[0]` is the ZERO ARTICLE, stated as the third article after L1-M1's
`a`/`an` and L1-M3's `the`, both of which were taught on countable things: a noun naming a whole
class or an idea in general takes nothing, and `the` appears only when what follows narrows the
class. The narrowing half is not left as theory — S01's third variation and pool item C12 are both
`The freedom to speak is important.`, the same noun with and without the article in the same
sentence pair.

`rules[1]` is the interference, and the brief was right that it runs the wrong way from what a
teacher expects: the learner does not omit `the`, they OVER-supply it, because forty modules of
countable practice have taught them that an English noun wants a word in front of it. Six of the
ten `mistake` rows are that error — `The freedom is important`, `The money is not the happiness`,
`the truth … the money`, `in the life`, `the society`, `The human nature` — which is the highest
concentration of one error in any module of this level, and deliberately so.

`rules[2]` is the HEDGE (`in general`, `on the whole`, `most people`, `some people`, `tend to`,
`it depends`), stated as grammar rather than manners because every sentence here is a general
statement and an unhedged one reads as a pronouncement. `rules[3]` is the impersonal subject —
Hindi's कोई / इंसान / आदमी pulling the learner to `One should do his duty`, which S05's `mistake`
carries verbatim with the honest verdict that it is not wrong but a century old. `rules[4]` does
what `docs/106` §3.5 asked: it NAMES L3-M1's stative-verb rule as already on the board for
`believe`, `know`, `understand` and `mean`, and does not restate it; it also draws the boundary
with L5-M6 — this module states a view and gives a reason, M6 defends one against an objection.

Twenty rows against a cap of 25: six whole keys (`most people`, `some people`, `in general`,
`on the whole`, `tend to`, `it depends`) and fourteen bare words. The content is civic and personal
throughout and names no religion, as the brief required.

either brief is wrong about a specific surface because of it, but the count and the module in that
sentence are now false, and both wave paragraphs must be treated as dated rather than as facts.

**2. M3's index seam is the sharpest in the level and it survives intact.** Every claim in
`docs/106` §3.3(5) re-checked and still true today:

> `have been    L4-M7` · `has been    L4-M7` · `have    L1-M4` · `been    L4-M3` · `never    L1-M4`
> `sound    L2-M6` · `sounds    L2-M6` · `queue    L4-M7` · `film    L1-M5` · `village    L4-M8`

The fix the brief prescribed is the one that shipped: `have you ever` (3) and `have never` (2) are

longest-match-first and the three- and two-token keys win at those positions. `been` resolves to
L4-M3's participle row on its own, which is true of the sentence.

**3. M3's fresh list is short the two words the brief's own examples need.** `docs/106` §3.3(2)

> `hear    free`

Both are genuinely unowned after forty-two modules, and this module opens them as one row

**4. The brief names the adjectives and the displays need the nouns.** §3.3(5) lists `British` and

So `Britain` and `America` are each ONE row carrying the adjective on its `forms` — which is also
the only safe shape, because two rows (`Britain` and `British`) would be two keys and a second row
opening a key the first already earned is exactly the `COLLIDES INSIDE THIS MODULE` class. And
`Indian English` costs one key, not two: `English` is L1-M1's and only `Indian` is spent.

**5. The one deliberate RE-TEACH in the wave, and why it stays.** `content:shown` reports:

> `RE-TEACH L5-M3-S07 "realise": L4-M10 owns the key`

This is the brief's own instruction executed (`docs/106` §3.3(5): "the spelling pair is one row

meaning — and tapping `realize` is shown this module's row — the spelling pair. Both notes are true

none.

**6. `write` is unowned, so the spelling pairs are stated with `is`.** `docs/106` §3.3's fourth

### Open questions for the native-speaker gate

Continuing the chain; the highest number before this wave was 135.

136. **`have you ever` and `have never` as unbreakable keys** (M3-S01, S05). The module indexes both
     whole so that no learner's tap reaches L4-M7's perfect passive. The cost is that the learner
     never meets `has he ever`, `have they ever` or `I have not ever` as taught forms. Confirm that
     teaching the two commonest shapes as fixed openings is the right trade at this level, and that
     a learner who has only these two will not produce `Have you ever been` where `Has she ever
     been` is wanted.

137. **A finished time stated as making the experience perfect impossible** (M3, `rules[1]`, S01 and
     S05 `mistake`). The rule says `yesterday`, `last year` and `in 2019` force the past simple.
     Confirm this is safe as an absolute for an Indian learner, given that Indian English quite
     regularly says `I have gone there last year` and the learner will hear it from people they
     trust.

138. **`film` against `movie` carried as a generational pair rather than a regional one** (M3-S03).
     The display is `My grandmother's generation says film, and my generation says movie.` and the
     row's note adds the regional reading (`film` British and Indian, `movie` American). Confirm
     that in India the generational split is real — that a twenty-year-old Hindi-English speaker
     genuinely says `movie` where a grandparent says `film` — rather than this being an American
     pattern imported into an Indian example.

139. **`Indian English is local English, and it is not wrong.`** (M3-S09). This is the module's
     premise as a sentence a learner will say aloud. Confirm it reads as affirmation rather than as
     defensiveness, and that `local` is the right adjective — the alternative `Indian English is a
     variety` needed a key the module did not have, and `local` may carry an unintended smallness.

140. **Mixing, not choosing, named as what marks an outsider** (M3-S10 and `rules[2]`). The claim is
     that an English reader forgives `colour` or `color` consistently but notices both in one
     letter. Confirm this is true of the readers an Indian learner actually writes to, where Indian
     usage already mixes British spelling with American vocabulary as a matter of course.

141. **`slang` glossed as ग़ैर-रस्मी and explicitly separated from गाली** (M3-S04, `trap`). Confirm
     that the Hindi word most learners will reach for is not स्लैंग meaning abuse, and that
     "बोलचाल का ग़ैर-रस्मी शब्द" is the gloss that prevents the confusion rather than one that
     creates it.

142. **`accent` and `dialect` named only inside a Hindi rule and shown nowhere** (M3, `rules[4]`).
     The course has no audio, so an accent cannot be practised. Confirm that naming the two words
     without teaching them as surfaces is more useful than leaving them out entirely — a learner who
     reads the rule may want to use the words and will find no row.

143. **`a few words` as a promise about length** (M4-S01, and the `mistake` on dropping `a`). The
     note says the phrase genuinely means "this will be short". Confirm that a speaker who then
     talks for ten minutes has broken something an English audience actually registers, which is the
     claim the row rests on.

144. **`Please join me in a toast. Raise a glass to the couple.`** (M4-S03). The display was written
     as two sentences so that both the fixed opening and the bare imperative are shown inside the
     twelve-word bound. Confirm this is how a toast is actually given, and in particular whether
     `Raise a glass` as an imperative to the room is natural or whether only
     `Please join me in raising a glass` (the first variation) is said.

145. **`I'm` permitted in the condolence while the module bans contractions** (M4-S04 and
     `rules[3]`). The rule states the exception explicitly: the ceremonial body takes no
     contractions, but `I'm so sorry for your loss` is a frozen whole and is said with one. Confirm
     the exception is real, and that `I am so sorry for your loss` (the first variation) is not the
     more usual form in writing.

146. **Stopping after the condolence, stated as an absolute** (M4-S04 `trap` and `mistake`,
     `rules[3]`). The module says nothing about the illness, the hospital or the will of God
     follows, and its `mistake` row is a learner asking `What was the illness?`. Confirm the
     absolute is the right teaching for a learner whose instinct is to narrate — this is the
     module's most consequential single claim and softening it would remove the protection it
     exists to give.

147. **`our` taught for the first time in this course at L5-M4** (M4-S07). Seam 7 above records that
     the possessive of `we` is unowned after forty-two modules. This module buys it inside a
     ceremonial sentence, so the learner's first `our` arrives glossed as the speech register's
     replacement for `my`. Confirm that gloss does not mislead — `our` is an ordinary everyday word
     and the note must not leave the learner thinking it is formal.

148. **`condolences` marked as more written than spoken** (M4-S08, row note). The note tells the
     learner to write `My condolences` and to say `I'm so sorry for your loss`. Confirm the split is
     that clean in practice, and that `My condolences` spoken aloud at a funeral is stiff rather
     than simply formal.

149. **`honour` carrying `honor` on its `forms`** (M4-S09). This makes a fourth British/American
     pair, taught in the ceremonial module rather than in L5-M3 where the other three live. Confirm
     that pointing at the pair here — rather than only in M3 — helps rather than splits the pattern,
     and that the silent `h` note (`an honour`) is the fact a learner most needs at that row.

150. **The over-supplied `the` called the most recognised marker of Indian English** (M5,
     `rules[1]`, and six of the ten `mistake` rows). The module states this as fact rather than as
     preference. Confirm the strength is right, and that a learner will not read six corrections of
     the same article as the course telling them their own English is broken — `docs/88` §1's
     standing rule is what M3 protects and this rule sits close to its edge.

151. **`The freedom to speak is important.` as the one licensed `the`** (M5-S01 variation and C12).
     The contrast pair is the module's proof that `the` is not banned but conditioned. Confirm that
     the narrowing which licenses it is visible to a learner from this one pair, or whether a second
     pair (`the money you gave me`, named in `rules[0]` but shown nowhere) is needed as a display.

152. **`tend to` taught as a hedge rather than as a statement about habit** (M5-S05, row note).
     The note says explicitly that `tend to` softens a general claim and does not describe a
     habit. Confirm the distinction survives contact with how the phrase is actually heard, since
     `People tend to forget` does also describe what people repeatedly do.

153. **`duty` and `justice` chosen as the module's value nouns** (M5-S08, S10). The brief required
     the content stay civic and personal rather than doctrinal, so no religion is named and धर्म is
     not translated anywhere. Confirm that `duty` glossed as कर्तव्य / फ़र्ज़ and `justice` as
     इंसाफ़ / न्याय reads as civic rather than as a quiet religious frame to a Hindi speaker, which
     is the failure mode the constraint exists to prevent.
