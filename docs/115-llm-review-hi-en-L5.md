# hi-en L5 — LLM review

This file records the LLM review of the hi-en fifth level, module by module, in the wave that
authored each one. It is the first L5 review doc for this course, so it opens here rather than
continuing an earlier file; `docs/106-hi-en-L5-brief-decisions.md` is the brief it is written
against, and its numbered open questions are continued rather than restarted.

**The index this wave was written against.** Every ownership claim below was produced by
`npm run content:owner -- hi-en "<surface>" …` on 2026-09-08, and every run in this wave closed
with the same footer:

> `1055 surfaces owned, folded over 40 modules through L4-M10`

So the fold is exactly the one `docs/106` §3 was checked against — 1055 surfaces over forty
modules, L1-M1 through L4-M10, with no L5 module of any course yet emitted. The seam the brief
warned about (`docs/106` §3.1(5): an index seam names a count and a module, and both go stale) had
not moved by the time these two modules were written. That is the only wave in this course for
which that will be true, and the next wave must re-run the check rather than quote this paragraph.

## Wave 1 — L5-M1, L5-M2 (#575)

### L5-M1 "Sayings and idioms" — the fixed phrase is one lexical item wearing several words

The ten displays:

1. `This expression is an idiom. Do not take it literally.`
2. `The exam was a piece of cake.`
3. `She called me out of the blue.`
4. `We go to the market once in a while.`
5. `Now and then I think about my old school.`
6. `Sooner or later he will tell you.`
7. `I know it by heart. I do not need the book.`
8. `He did not do it on purpose. Never mind, it was just bad luck.`
9. `Hang on, I am coming. I am not late.`
10. `I am fed up with this work. Take it easy, he said.`

**What it teaches.** The FIXED PHRASE, in the brief's three testable parts, one per rule. `rules[0]`
is the no-synonym law (`a piece of cake`, never `a slice of cake`); `rules[1]` is the frozen
internal grammar (`once in a time` and `out of the blues` are both broken, and so is
`a very big piece of cake` — an idiom is not made emphatic by lengthening it, which is the brief's
quieter second interference and is carried again by S02's `mistake`); `rules[2]` is the part-of-speech
slot, which is what makes `a piece of cake` a noun phrase after `be`, `once in a while` and
`now and then` adverbials, and `never mind` · `hang on` · `take it easy` whole turns.
`rules[3]` is the Hindi pair the brief asked for: the Hindi idiom, the literal English it produces,
and the English idiom that does the job — `सर मत खाओ` → `do not eat my head`, `दिमाग़ खा गया` →
`he ate my brain`, `बाएँ हाथ का खेल` → `a game of the left hand`, whose real English is
`a piece of cake`. `rules[4]` carries the second half of the job line: these are neutral-to-informal
speech and none belongs in L4-M7's official register, and it is where the two five-token idioms
(`once in a blue moon`, `off the top of my head`) are NAMED without being shown.

S01 is the frame sentence and is deliberately meta: it opens `expression`, `idiom` and `literally`
so that the nine sentences after it have a word for what they are doing. Sentence 8 is the only
three-row sentence, because `never mind` reads best as the answer to `on purpose` and `bad luck`
is the reason it is offered.

The module is `informal` on S02, S08, S09 and S10 and `neutral` elsewhere — the register chip is
doing the "and when it is used" half of the job line at the item level, and `hang on` is the one
whose `trap` bars it from writing and from official language outright.

Fifteen new keys against a cap of 25: eleven whole phrases (`a piece of cake`, `out of the blue`,
`once in a while`, `now and then`, `sooner or later`, `by heart`, `on purpose`, `never mind`,
`hang on`, `fed up`, `take it easy`) and four bare words (`idiom`, `expression`, `literally`,
`luck`), plus the two plurals `idioms` and `expressions` on their own rows' `forms`.

### L5-M2 "Humour and teasing" — English flags a joke with words, and the flags have a fixed place

The ten displays:

1. `I'm only joking. Do not take it the wrong way.`
2. `That was a silly question — just kidding.`
3. `No offence, but this shop is not very good.`
4. `That was a joke. I did not mean it.`
5. `He is very funny, but he is never serious.`
6. `Do not tease him. He does not take a joke.`
7. `Come on, do not take it seriously.`
8. `The food was not bad at all. She was very happy.`
9. `I meant it as a joke. She was offended.`
10. `Think about her feelings. Do not offend her.`

**What it teaches.** The MARKED JOKE. `rules[0]` names the closed set and its two positions —
`no offence, but …` before, `I'm only joking` · `just kidding` after — and says the flag is made of
WORDS, not of delivery. `rules[1]` is the position law itself, and it is the one the module tests
twice: S02's `mistake` is `Just kidding, that was a silly question`, a flag moved to the front,
which stops being a flag and becomes a warning. `rules[2]` is understatement as warmth, which S08
carries: `not bad at all` is praise and the `trap` says so in as many words, because a learner reads
it as lukewarm and answers as though criticised. `rules[3]` is the brief's three interferences —
deadpan delivery, personal remarks, and the seniority licence that `आप`/`तुम` marks and English
cannot — and the personal-remarks half is stated as a fact about the audience, never as a fault in
the learner, in S03's and S10's `trap` and in S06's. `rules[4]` is the split with L5-M7, written
explicitly so the two modules do not blur: this module owns friendly irony that is marked and
immediately defused, L5-M7 owns unmarked implication and sarcasm. `rules[4]` is also where `hurt`
is named without being shown (see the seams below).

The repair is one short fixed line and not an apology, as the brief required: S04's
`I did not mean it` and S09's `I meant it as a joke` are the whole of it, and S04's `trap` says
plainly that `I did not mean it` is not an apology — it only clears up the intention.

Twenty-one new keys against a cap of 25: eight whole phrases (`only joking`, `the wrong way`,
`just kidding`, `no offence`, `mean it`, `take a joke`, `come on`, `not bad at all`) and thirteen
bare surfaces across nine rows (`silly`, `joke`/`jokes`/`joking`, `funny`, `serious`/`seriously`,
`tease`/`teasing`, `meant`, `offended`/`offend`, `feelings`).

### Brief seams corrected, and what the emitted index actually said

**1. The seam that survived: the module called `Sayings and idioms` still may not write `saying`.**
`docs/106` §3.1(2) is the loudest claim in the brief and it is still true today:

> `saying    L2-M7`

Neither module writes it. `sayings` in the plural came back `free`, and was left alone for the
brief's own reason — opening the plural leaves an unreachable singular beside it. S01 uses
`expression` (`free`) and `idiom` (`free`) instead, and its `trap` tells the learner why in
Devanagari, which is the only place in the course the word `saying` appears at all.

**2. `not bad at all` swallows an OWNED key, and the brief's swallow list does not say so.**
`docs/106` §3.5 names four phrases that exist only to protect a bare word from a false note —
`mean it`, `never mind`, `what do you call`, `the opposite of`. It is short by at least one:

> `not bad    L1-M2`
> `at all    free`

`not bad` is L1-M2's — the `Not bad` of the how-are-you exchange, which means "ठीक-ठाक" — and
`not bad at all` is praise. Longest-match-first means the four-token key wins wherever the whole
phrase is written, so a learner tapping S08 is shown the praise note and not the lukewarm one. The
row's `note` names L1-M2 explicitly so the pair is visible rather than hidden, and the sentence's
`trap` tells the learner not to hear the praise as a cold answer. This was found by asking `content:owner` about the phrase's PARTS, not the phrase — the
brief's fresh-and-whole list had already told the truth about the phrase.

**3. Three more swallows the brief does not list, all in M2.** Same check, same shape:

> `way    L4-M1`  ·  `just    L1-M8`  ·  `come    L1-M6`

`the wrong way` swallows L4-M1's `way` (its "how" adverbial), `just kidding` swallows L1-M8's
`just`, and `come on` swallows L1-M6's `come`. None is a defect and none needed a change; all three
are recorded because the brief's swallow list reads as exhaustive and is not. Each row's `note`
points back at the owner.

**4. In M1 the swallow is the rule rather than the exception, and the brief names only one of them.**
`docs/106` §3.5 records `never mind` swallowing L1-M4's `never`. The index says nine of the eleven
phrases do the same thing:

> `piece    L2-M3`  ·  `blue    L2-M3`  ·  `while    L3-M10`  ·  `by    L2-M4`  ·  `on    L1-M4`
> ·  `never    L1-M4`  ·  `later    L4-M6`  ·  `now    L3-M6`  ·  `then    L1-M10`

`a piece of cake` covers L2-M3's `piece`, `out of the blue` covers L2-M3's `blue`,
`once in a while` covers L3-M10's `while`, `by heart` covers L2-M4's directional `by`, `on purpose`
covers L1-M4's `on`, `sooner or later` covers L4-M6's `later`, and `now and then` covers both
L3-M6's `now` and L1-M10's `then`. The consequence is a design constraint the brief does not state
and this module obeys everywhere: **no display in L5-M1 writes any of those words bare.** The only
free parts of these phrases — `sooner`, `once`, `out`, `hang`, `fed`, `purpose`, `heart`, `or`,
`easy`, `cake` — are never shown alone either, so the module opens no bare key it did not intend to.

**5. Three of M1's seven offered bare keys were deliberately left unspent, and `moon` could not be
spent.** The brief offers `idiom`, `expression`, `literally`, `luck`, `mind`, `moon`, `cake`. All
seven came back `free` and four were taken. `cake` is redundant once `a piece of cake` is whole.
`mind` is left free on purpose: `docs/106` §3.5 gives L5-M7 the whole key `I don't mind`, and a bare
`mind` opened here for "never mind" would be a second job on a word M7 will want. **`moon` was
unspendable by the span decision**, and this is the first module to hit that wall in practice:
`once in a blue moon` is five tokens, `maxSpan` stays 4 for the whole course, and a four-token
prefix would resolve `once in a blue` to nothing. It is therefore NAMED in `rules[4]` in Devanagari
and never shown, which is exactly the mechanism `docs/106` §3.4 describes for a blocked surface —
`tools/check-shown.ts` does not scan `rules[].text`.

**6. `hurt` stayed blocked and moved into a rule, as the M2 brief required.** `content:owner`:

> `hurt    L3-M7`

That is the body row. A feeling that is hurt is a second job on it, so no M2 display writes the
word; `rules[4]` names it in Devanagari and hands the job to L5-M7 along with the sarcasm. The
module says `She was offended` instead, on a fresh row whose `trap` draws the line between
`angry` (L1-M9, ग़ुस्सा) and `offended` (अपमान का बुरा मानना).

**7. `right` stayed blocked too, and was never needed.** `right → L2-M4` is the direction word
(`docs/106` §3.4), and M2's subject invites `That's right` constantly. No display writes it; the
module's agreement move is `not bad at all`, which is what the brief actually asked for.

**8. The hyphen seam did not bite, because no display in either module contains a hyphen.**
Re-checked today and unchanged:

> `light-hearted    free   [parts: light → L2-M3, hearted → free]`
> `old-fashioned    free   [parts: old → L4-M8, fashioned → free]`

The M2 brief's suggested replacement (`light and easy`) was not needed either — `funny`, `silly`
and `just kidding` cover the ground, and neither `light` nor `and easy` appears. Recorded so the
next wave knows the seam is still live and still unspent.

**9. Four ownership claims in the M1 brief that were checked and held, with nothing to correct.**
`mean` and `means → L3-M1`, `blue → L2-M3`, `cost → L1-M8`, `rain → L3-M4`, `really → L1-M10`,
`never → L1-M4`. None of these words is written by either module. They are listed because a claim
that survives the check is worth as much to the next wave as one that does not.

### The ratchet

`npm run content:shown -- hi-en L5-M1` and `… L5-M2` both print
`clean — every shown surface resolves`, with **no `RE-TEACH` line and no `COLLIDES INSIDE THIS
MODULE` line on either module**. There is nothing to explain away here: neither module opens a key
an earlier module owns, and no two rows of a module open the same folded key.

`npx vitest run tools/shown-surfaces.test.ts` is 11/11 and hi-en holds at exactly its baseline of
**30**. The two modules contribute **zero** findings — the distinct set is unchanged and is still
the same thirty surfaces (`priya`, `mumbai`, `jaipur`, `doctor`, `farmer`, `actor`, `coffee`,
`water`, `cricket`, `films`, `dogs`, `hindi`, `how's`, `it`, `going`, `and`, `so`, `today`, `speak`,
`milk`, `please`, `three`, `six`, `reading`, `new`, `isn't`, `now`, `ate`, `me`, `bus`). No baseline
was raised and none could honestly be lowered by this wave: every surface on that list belongs to
L1–L4 content that a level never edits.

`npm run content:validate` reports `hi-en/L5-M1.json ok` and `hi-en/L5-M2.json ok`.

`npx vitest run src/course/types.test.ts` fails only on the two module-census assertions — the
360-module list and `expect(hiEn.length).toBe(40)` — which belong to whoever lands the level, not to
this wave. Because the hi-en count aborts the walk beneath it, the #270 language law was re-run
directly over the two new files instead: `display`, word `display`, `forms`, `mistake.display`,
`variations[].display` and pool `display` carry no Devanagari; `cue`, `literal`, `sound`, `usage`,
`mnemonic`, `trap`, `mistake.why`, `variations[].cue`, `variations[].changed`, every word `cue` and
every word `note` do; no `glossEn` is present anywhere; no `’` appears in any display; and every
`note` is under the 200-character ceiling. `L5-M2`'s `minWordsPerSentence` is 2 rather than 3
because its shortest taught item, the bare flag `Just kidding.`, is two words — which is the module
teaching brevity as content, not a bound quietly slipping.

### Open questions for the native-speaker gate

Continuing the chain in `docs/106-hi-en-L5-brief-decisions.md`, whose highest number is 119.

120. **`a piece of cake` as the plate for `बाएँ हाथ का खेल`** (M1, `rules[3]`). The rule pairs three
     Hindi idioms with the nonsense their literal English produces and gives the English idiom that
     really does the job. Confirm `a piece of cake` is the phrase an Indian English speaker would
     actually reach for here rather than `child's play`, which is closer to the Hindi vehicle and
     which the module does not teach.

121. **`out of the blue` for a phone call** (M1-S03). Confirm the idiom is neutral about whether the
     surprise was welcome. The Hindi cue is `उसने अचानक ही मुझे फ़ोन कर दिया`, which reads slightly
     pleased; if the English leans negative the cue needs to lean with it.

122. **`once in a while` against `now and then`** (M1-S04, S05). The module teaches them as
     interchangeable adverbials with one Hindi gloss each (`कभी-कभी`, `कभी-कभार`). Confirm there is
     no frequency difference a learner would be heard getting wrong, and that the two Hindi glosses
     are not themselves distinguishable in a way English does not support.

123. **`by heart` glossed as `ज़बानी`** (M1-S07). Confirm `ज़बानी याद` is the everyday Hindi rather
     than `कंठस्थ`, which the `cue` also offers and which may read as bookish to a learner whose
     Hindi is colloquial.

124. **`never mind` as a reply to someone else's mistake** (M1-S08). The module places it after
     `He did not do it on purpose` and the `trap` says it is not an apology. Confirm it does not
     read as dismissive when the mistake was real, and that `कोई बात नहीं` is the right pairing
     rather than `छोड़िए`.

125. **`hang on` barred from writing** (M1-S09, `trap`). The trap says it never appears on paper or
     in official language. Confirm the line is drawn in the right place for Indian English, where
     `hang on` may be more written than the brief assumes.

126. **`fed up with`, and whether `of` is heard** (M1-S10). The module teaches `with` and marks
     `from` as the Hindi `से` showing through. Indian English also has `fed up of`. Confirm whether
     that variant is common enough that the `trap` should name it as a third option to avoid rather
     than leaving it unmentioned.

127. **`take it easy` as a calming line rather than a farewell** (M1-S10). The module teaches only
     the calming sense. Confirm the goodbye sense is rare enough in the learner's English that
     leaving it out is safe, since a learner who meets it as a farewell has no row to reach.

128. **The em dash before `just kidding`** (M2-S02). The display is
     `That was a silly question — just kidding.` Confirm the dash is what a speaker's pause becomes
     in writing, and that a comma would not be the ordinary choice — the module never teaches the
     dash as punctuation, it only shows it.

129. **`no offence, but` and whether the flag actually helps** (M2-S03). The module teaches it as a
     working flag and its `trap` says it does NOT license a personal remark. Confirm English hearers
     accept `no offence, but` on an opinion about a thing (a shop, a piece of work), rather than
     hearing it as a signal that something rude is coming regardless.

130. **`I did not mean it` as intention-clearing and not apology** (M2-S04, `trap`). This is the
     module's whole repair and the brief forbade an apology at length. Confirm a native hearer
     treats the line as sufficient in a light exchange, and that `sorry` is genuinely optional after
     it.

131. **`take a joke` in the negative only** (M2-S06). The module teaches `He does not take a joke`
     and never the positive `He can take a joke`. Confirm the positive is ordinary enough that its
     absence is a gap worth filling in a later wave, or rare enough that the negative alone is right.

132. **`not bad at all` as full praise** (M2-S08). This is the module's most consequential claim and
     the one a learner is likeliest to mis-hear. Confirm it is praise rather than faint approval in
     ordinary speech, and confirm that `thank you` is the expected reply — the `trap` states both.

133. **`offended` against `angry`** (M2-S09, `trap`). The line drawn is ग़ुस्सा against
     अपमान का बुरा मानना. Confirm the distinction survives in the learner's Hindi, where
     `बुरा मान गया` may cover both.

134. **Deadpan delivery stated as a fact about the audience** (M2, `rules[3]`). The rule says an
     English joke is often said flat, and that the Hindi laugh cue reads as trying too hard.
     Confirm this generalisation is fair across the varieties an Indian learner will actually meet,
     rather than true of one of them.

135. **The personal-remarks rule as an absolute** (M2, `rules[3]` and S03, S06, S10 `trap`). Weight,
     skin, marriage and salary are stated as not jokes in English at ANY level of friendship.
     Confirm the absolute is the right teaching even where it is not quite the truth — the brief
     called this the module's most useful single sentence, and softening it would cost the learner
     the protection it is there to give.
